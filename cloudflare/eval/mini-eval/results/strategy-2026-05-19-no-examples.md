# The Bet — Bearaby
*Prepared 2026-05-19*

---

## 1. The Memo

Bearaby has a genuinely strong product and a proven ability to sell it — through warm channels where the buyer already half-knows they want this kind of thing before they meet the brand. PR, design press, organic social, retail: those channels work because the buyer is pre-warmed. Meta cold traffic is a colder sport. A stranger in a scroll has never heard of Bearaby and stops only when an ad catches them on something *specific to their situation*.

Bearaby's paid history — boosted organic lifestyle shots — doesn't do that. The imagery is beautiful, but it looks like every competitor's Meta feed: calm, cozy, generic "rest and wellness." It gives the hot sleeper, the ADHD adult, and the anxious insomniac no signal that this product was made for their particular problem. At $199+, generic isn't enough to earn the click. That's the blocker, and it's fixable.

But we don't yet know *which* specific buyer converts at a profitable CAC in cold paid. The founder named this gap directly. Before we sharpen messages, we need to find the audience. Round 1 is an audience-discovery test: three parallel bets on three distinct buyer identities. The winner scales and becomes the foundation for Round 2 message variants. The losers are as valuable — they tell us where not to put the budget.

One practical adaptation on the budget: $25K/month split across three ad sets puts estimated weekly purchase volume per cell below Meta's learning-phase exit threshold (the math is in the flag section below). We recommend starting each cell on add-to-cart optimisation for weeks 1–2 to build signal faster, then switching all three to purchase optimisation for weeks 3–8. Judge each cell on cumulative cost-per-purchase over the full eight-week run — not on early-week CPAs during learning.

One precondition before launch: angle-specific landing pages, or at minimum angle-specific above-the-fold treatments on the Napper PDP. A hot-sleeper ad that lands on a generic homepage leaks. It's a two-day build. Do it first.

---

## 2. Conversion Event

A completed online purchase at bearaby.com. Lead product: the Napper weighted blanket.

---

## 3. The Blocker

Bearaby's paid creative to date speaks in the same voice as the entire competitive field — warm, cozy lifestyle imagery that signals nothing specific to any particular buyer. A cold stranger with a named problem (overheating at night, a nervous system that won't switch off, sensory overwhelm) gets no hook that this product is made for *them*, and at $199+ there is no impulse — only a reason to click. The brand has multiple genuine, differentiated product advantages; the discovery test is designed to find which buyer-advantage pairing produces that reason.

---

## 4. The Angles

### Angle 1 — The Hot Sleeper Who's Been Burned Before

**Hypothesis:** Buyers who found pellet-filled weighted blankets too hot — or who have avoided the category for that reason — are a high-conversion cold audience for Bearaby's filler-free, breathable construction. This person has a prior, specific frustration that Bearaby directly resolves. An ad can name it explicitly and let the product be the proof.

**Why:** Reviews in the research confirm breathability as Bearaby's most-cited differentiator versus pellet-filled competitors; it is not a marketing claim but a literal design fact. No premium competitor owns this position — the field is pellet-filled, and Bearaby is structurally alone here. Timing reinforces the hypothesis: it is late May heading into summer, which is the counter-season use case the research identifies for hot sleepers. The audience is self-identifying (anyone who has woken up sweating under a blanket) and large enough to carry the spend.

**Visual lane:** Product-forward, bright and breezy — a summer-morning register, not a winter-cocoon one. The Napper draped over or around a person in a well-lit, warm-season setting, with the knit texture and open construction visible and central (the product's breathability must be *visible*, not asserted). Direct text overlay naming the frustration. Stay within Bearaby's warm, natural-light identity — shift only the season and the energy.

---

### Angle 2 — The Neurodivergent Adult (ADHD / Sensory Processing)

**Hypothesis:** ADHD adults and adults with sensory processing differences are a large, socially active, self-identified US audience who use weighted products for grounding — but Bearaby does not speak to them explicitly, and no premium competitor holds this space at the adult end. This is a genuine gap in a category where Harkla and SensaCalm address the kids/family version but leave adult sensory-seekers unclaimed.

**Why:** The research lists neurodivergent households as a confirmed real Bearaby buyer segment — the product is already being purchased for this purpose without any targeted marketing. The ADHD adult community is substantial and has high social-media engagement, giving the self-selection trigger ("your nervous system is always on") the volume to be testable at this budget. The brand's premium, design-led product is a natural fit: this audience is not looking for a clinical apparatus, they want something that works and that they are not embarrassed to have in their home.

**Visual lane:** Personal and intimate — a real adult (not a model, or a model shot in a way that reads as real) wrapped fully in the Napper on a sofa or chair. The compression and enveloping quality of the knit are the focal point, not the room. UGC-adjacent tone: warm, first-person energy, the sense of being held rather than styled. Remain within Bearaby's earthy palette and natural light — but shift the composition from lifestyle tableau to person-being-held.

---

### Angle 3 — The Anxious Person Who Cannot Switch Off

**Hypothesis:** Anxiety-driven poor sleep is the category's broadest claimed use case and the most competitive lane. The question this cell answers is: *can Bearaby win here despite the noise?* If yes — if the brand's specific credentials (the sensory pressure, the internal outcome statistics, the design) convert cold against a crowded field — this is a large market to scale into. If no — if cost-per-purchase is materially worse than angles 1 or 2 — that is the most actionable finding of the test: go specific, not broad.

**Why:** Every competitor runs "calm and sleep" Meta creative (confirmed in the competitor research). Bearaby has been doing the same with boosted posts. This cell is the baseline — it tells us exactly how much the specificity of angles 1 and 2 is worth relative to the category's default positioning. The internal customer survey figures (85% report better sleep, 60% feel less anxious) are available as proof-point assets, clearly labelled as from a Bearaby customer survey.

**Visual lane:** Quiet, restful, nighttime-adjacent. The Napper on a bed or draped over a person with eyes closed — low drama, permission-to-rest energy, muted earthy palette. Text or spoken hook about the loop of thoughts before sleep. This is intentionally closest to Bearaby's existing organic content — so the test can cleanly measure whether that familiar look converts cold, rather than conflating visual novelty with audience response.

---

## 5. The Honest Flag

**Budget and learning-phase math:** At $25K/month across three ad sets (~$278/day per cell), estimated weekly purchase volume per cell is approximately 25–35 (using an industry-benchmark estimated CPA of $65–$80 for a ~$210 AOV DTC home-goods product — this is an estimate, not the founder's confirmed figure). That sits below Meta's 50-conversion-per-week learning exit threshold. The mitigation: begin on add-to-cart optimisation (higher volume event, estimated cost $15–$25) for weeks 1–2 to exit learning, then shift all cells to purchase for weeks 3–8. Over an eight-week window, cumulative purchase signal per cell will be readable. Do not judge on week-2 CPAs.

**Price is a non-creative ceiling, not a reason to discount:** At $199–$299 in a category anchored by $30–$50 Amazon alternatives, the price must be *earned in the ad* — by making the specific value legible (filler-free breathability, real materials, a product designed to be displayed). Do not run introductory discounts during the discovery test. Discounting will depress the CPA number, make the test look better than it is, and undermine the premium positioning the brand has built through PR and design press. If the test returns a CPA that margin cannot sustain *at full price*, that is important and honest information — not a reason to paper over it with a promo.

**Precondition — landing page alignment:** Each angle needs a matched landing experience. A hot-sleeper ad landing on a generic Napper PDP that leads with sustainability messaging will leak. At minimum: angle-specific above-the-fold copy and hero image on the PDP for each of the three cells. This is a two-to-three day build and must be live before the test launches.

**Timing note — Father's Day (June 21, 2026):** Father's Day is 33 days away. Gift buyers are a real Bearaby segment (the product is premium, beautiful, and solves a problem a gift-giver wants to solve for someone they love). Run a small, separate gifting burst — suggested $2,000–$3,000, separate campaign — targeting people buying for a sleep-deprived or anxious person in their life. Time-box it: June 1–20. Keep it entirely separate from the discovery test. A gifting buyer is a different audience from the three above; folding them in will muddy the read.

# The Bet — Ever/Body NYC
**Prepared:** 2026-05-19

---

## 1. The Memo

Ever/Body has something the NYC medspa market almost never has, and is not currently advertising: a genuinely low-pressure, welcoming experience. Across hundreds of reviews, first-time patients volunteer — unprompted — that no one pushed them into anything, that consultations didn't feel salesy, that they finally found a place that didn't make them feel judged. In a category where hard upselling and fear of looking "overdone" are the two biggest reasons curious people stay stuck and never book, that proof is rare and commercially significant.

The problem is that your current ads look like everyone else's — before/afters and a percentage-off offer. A cold stranger scrolling Instagram cannot distinguish you from Skinney running "10 units free" or any other injector running a deal. You are marketing the category when you should be marketing the reason to choose *you*. The "world-class experts / gold standard" positioning on the website competes directly with SkinSpirit — a 20-year-old, surgeon-founded national chain — on their strongest ground, and loses. The story the brand can actually win on is sitting in the reviews.

The test below runs three angles, each a different expression of the same underlying truth: this is the safe place to take your first step. The angles test whether the conversion driver is the *experience* (you won't be pressured), the *price conversation* (you can afford to find out), or the *outcome* (you won't look done). That tells us which framing of the trust story moves cold NYC strangers to book a first appointment — because it is the one story that is both genuinely true and uncontested in the market.

One structural note before spending anything: at $8,000/month, the Meta conversion event should be a **consultation-request form submission**, not a completed booking. Completed bookings won't generate enough weekly volume for Meta's algorithm to learn across three ad sets at this budget. Lead forms will. The clinic team converts leads to bookings through its existing follow-up process — that part of the funnel already works.

---

## 2. Conversion Event

A submitted consultation-request form (a "lead") — the Meta-optimised event. The downstream business goal is a first booked appointment; the optimisation event is the form fill, because booking volume is insufficient at this budget for clean learning. The clinic's follow-up team closes the gap between form and chair.

---

## 3. The Blocker

A cold, qualified prospect encountering Ever/Body's current ads sees something indistinguishable from any other NYC medspa running before/afters and a discount. The category's two biggest consumer fears — being pressured into treatments you don't need, and leaving looking visibly "done" — go completely unaddressed. The brand has credible, organic, unprompted proof against both fears and is not using it. The blocker is the message, not the product.

---

## 4. The Angles

The 20% off first-visit offer is a standing new-patient promotion and should appear consistently across all three cells as a supporting CTA detail — *not* as the headline. Keeping it constant means the offer is not the variable being tested; the message framing is. This is deliberate: we need to know whether the brand story alone can move cold traffic, not whether a discount does.

---

### Angle A — The No-Pressure Room

**Hypothesis:** The primary conversion blocker for qualified first-timers is anxiety about being sold to. An ad that explicitly names and defuses that fear — "the medspa where no one pushes you into anything" — will outperform the current discount-led format with this audience.

**Why (anchored in evidence):** The single most consistent unprompted theme in Ever/Body's reviews across all NYC locations is that the experience is low-pressure and not salesy — reviewers use specific language like "never pressure to purchase" and "did not feel salesly" without being asked. This is not a generic brand claim; it is what first-time patients document after the fact. No competitor runs this message; the market clusters around authority credentials and deals. The category's own well-documented reputation for hard upselling means this is a point of real differentiation, not positioning inflation.

**Visual lane:** Warm, candid clinic photography — real providers with patients, relaxed and unhurried. Soft lighting in the contemporary space. The mood should read as *this is not the intimidating dermatologist's office* — belonging, not aspiration. No transformation imagery in this cell. The human interaction in the room is the product being shown.

---

### Angle B — What's It Actually Going to Cost?

**Hypothesis:** Pricing anxiety is a distinct pre-booking barrier — curious people don't book because they don't know if they can afford it and don't want to walk into a consultation unable to say yes. An ad that opens the cost conversation honestly ("here's roughly what to expect; we'll tell you exactly at your visit") will convert better than one leading with a percentage discount, by removing the uncertainty that stalls booking intent.

**Why (anchored in evidence):** Price is the single most common negative review theme — "they overcharge," "the price is extremely high." This signals that cost is on prospects' minds before they arrive as much as after. The website lists no prices; consultations are where cost is set. The brand's founding identity was explicitly built around price transparency — that DNA is retrievable. A hook that says "let's be straight about what this costs" is both true to the brand's origin and addresses a documented barrier. It is a distinct entry point from a discount ("here's a real number to reason from") rather than a vague deal.

**Visual lane:** Clean and text-prominent, anchored by a real provider photo or short direct-to-camera video clip. A calm, no-fluff tone — the provider speaks plainly, without jargon, about what to expect. No clinical imagery. The provider's real face carries the credibility; the copy carries the honesty. The feeling is: *a straight answer from someone who knows.*

**Precondition (see Flag 1 below):** This angle makes a promise the current destination may not keep. Before running Angle B, the consultation landing page needs one sentence of honest pricing context — e.g. "most first visits start at [X]; your exact plan is set at your consultation." Without that, the ad generates curiosity the site immediately disappoints.

---

### Angle C — You'll Look Like You

**Hypothesis:** Fear of looking "overdone" or unnatural is a distinct barrier from pressure anxiety — some prospects stay away because they've seen bad work on others, not because of sales anxiety. An ad leading with subtle, natural-looking results ("refreshed, not done") will convert this segment better than either experience or cost messaging.

**Why (anchored in evidence):** Fear of unnatural results is a widely-voiced category concern, and Ever/Body's real results — as documented across its reviews and available photography — skew natural-looking. The visual assets to prove this exist. Competitors run before/after ads too, but the standard category execution is dramatic transformation. A before/after that shows subtle, flattering improvement ("you look like yourself, just rested") is a different message even in the same format. This is the most conventional angle of the three — worth one slot because if it outperforms the trust-led angles, we've learned the audience is more outcome-driven than experience-driven.

**Visual lane:** Real before/after photography — real patients, subtle and natural transformation. The creative brief: the "after" must not look obviously "done." Side-by-side or a soft reveal treatment, warm colour tone, not clinical whites. The copy framing — "Results that look like you" — is the differentiator from the typical dramatic B/A the rest of the category runs. This is what makes the cell distinct from competitors, not the format.

---

## 5. The Honest Flags

**Flag 1 — Landing page gap (precondition for Angle B, worth fixing for all three):**
The pricing-transparency hook in Angle B will frustrate users who click through and find no pricing guidance on the destination page — potentially damaging the brand's honesty positioning rather than building it. Fix: add a single line of honest pricing context to the consultation landing page before launching. Something like: "Most first visits start at $[X]; your exact treatment plan and cost are set at your consultation — no obligation." This is a fast copy change and it protects both Angle B's conversion rate and the brand's credibility for all traffic.

**Flag 2 — Optimise for leads, not bookings:**
The conversion event Must be a form fill / consultation request, not a completed booking. At $8,000/month across three ad sets, completed bookings will not reach the volume Meta needs to exit its learning phase (~50 conversion events per week per ad set). Optimising for an event that rarely fires produces noise, not signal. A lead form generates enough volume for the algorithm to learn and for results to be comparable across angles. This is the correct structure for this budget — not a compromise.

**Flag 3 — Unit economics are unknown:**
The founder did not provide average first-visit ticket value or patient LTV. Without these, a precise viable CPL cannot be confirmed. Working from category context only (not a founder-confirmed figure — treat this as an estimate), a CPL under $50–70 would likely sustain healthy economics for a NYC cosmetic dermatology first visit; if early data shows CPL consistently above $100, revisit budget allocation and audience targeting before the full month is spent. The founder should share a first-visit ticket average before launch so a clear success threshold can be set. Running without one means judging the test on a number nobody agreed on in advance.

**Flag 4 — Timing note:**
Mid-May is a favourable window — the spring peak for cosmetic dermatology demand in NYC (pre-summer). Beginning now gives a clean 6–8 week read before summer travel patterns begin to fragment attention in mid-July. Start the test in the next two weeks to use the seasonal tailwind.

# The Bet — Verbis Edu
*Prepared 19 May 2026*

---

## 1. The Memo

Here is the real situation: Verbis Edu has spent 22 years building a genuine reputation — but it is a reputation for coaching, not for placing students abroad. The ~435 Justdial ratings are overwhelmingly about trainers and exam scores. The placement claims on the site ("2,400+ placed," "95% visa success rate") are self-stated, with no independent voice behind them. A cold stranger clicking a Meta ad for study-abroad consultancy arrives in a category where scam-warning content is everywhere — and when they look for proof, they find strong evidence of a coaching business and little verifiable evidence of a consultancy. That gap is why conversion will be suppressed, and no amount of clever creative fully fixes it. That fix has to run in parallel (see the flag below).

What creative *can* do is something the national competitors — Leverage Edu, Yocket, IDP — cannot replicate: Verbis Edu has named, reviewable human beings who coached students to their scores *and then* guided them to universities. That end-to-end pipeline is the brand's real differentiator. It cannot be said by a platform. It is also the single positioning thread that connects the three angles this test runs.

The timing is as good as it gets. It is mid-May 2026. Class 12 results are landing right now across India. The students who just got their scores are at their sharpest decision moment about what comes next. One of the three angles is built entirely around this window — it has a natural expiry by late July. Launch now.

What the test does not know — and must learn — is who the online converter actually is: the student who just finished exams, the parent who holds the money and the anxiety, or the OET-track healthcare professional who needs both a score and a work visa. These three audiences need different messages and represent genuinely different hypotheses. Round one answers that question.

Two things must be in place before any budget is spent: the Meta pixel on the /apply form, and a confirmed CPL target from the founder. Neither exists yet. Without them the test is unreadable.

## 2. Conversion Event

A submitted "Check Eligibility" form on /apply, recorded as a Meta lead — resulting in a booked 30-minute counselling call with a Verbis Edu counsellor.

## 3. The Blocker

A cold stranger from a Meta ad arrives at a study-abroad consultancy in a category with a documented fraud reputation. The brand's placement claims are entirely self-stated; its real third-party proof base (coaching reviews) is for a different service. There is no independent voice confirming the consultancy works. In the absence of that, a cautious student or parent does not submit the form.

## 4. The Angles

---

### Angle A — The Exam-Fresh Student: Score to Seat

**Hypothesis:** A student who has just received Class 12 or undergraduate results, and is now urgently deciding what comes next, is the highest-intent audience available in this test window — and they convert when the message meets them at that exact moment and shows Verbis as the one place that handles both the exam score and the university seat.

**Why:** The research confirms that Verbis Edu's actual student pipeline begins with coaching. That is not a weakness — it is the brand's sharpest structural advantage over any pure consultancy. A student who sees "we coach you to your IELTS score, then we get you your university seat" hears a promise that IDP and Leverage Edu cannot credibly make in the same breath. The timing sharpens this further: May 2026 is the precise post-exam window when this audience is making live decisions. The trigger ("you just got your results — here's what happens next") is as sharp as it gets for Meta self-selection. This angle has a natural expiry by late July; it must run now.

**Visual lane:** Real photo — a student in their early twenties, warm but unposed, holding a score report or admit letter visibly in frame. The document is doing the trust work; it is evidence, not decoration. Expression is calm and forward-looking, not triumphant. Clean layout, Verbis branding present but not dominant. Copy energy: *"You've got your score. Now, your university."* Link to the eligibility check form.

---

### Angle B — The Parent: 22 Years, Real People, Not a Platform

**Hypothesis:** The actual financial decision-maker for undergraduate study abroad is the parent — and in a category where parents are actively warned to distrust consultants, the conversion trigger is not aspiration but verifiable proof of legitimacy: named human beings, a physical address, and a track record that cannot be faked.

**Why:** Competitors cluster on scale signals — students placed, university partner counts, AI tools — that any bad actor can also claim, and that parents have been told to distrust. Verbis Edu's 22-year operating history at the same Bangalore centres, with named trainers whose reviews are publicly searchable, is a fundamentally different trust signal. It is a fact. Facebook's audience skews toward parents, not students. This is the right channel for a message that says: *this is a real organisation with real people you can hold accountable* — and that message is the one thing the national platforms genuinely cannot copy.

**Visual lane:** Named counsellor — Parthasarathy Bhaskara or Vinutha, whichever is willing to be on camera — facing the viewer directly, professional but warm, against the actual centre environment (real walls, real furniture, not a studio backdrop). No stock photography anywhere in this cell. The named person and the real location are the entire trust argument. Copy names both the years and the person: *"22 years. The same team. Book a real conversation."* The visual distinctness from Angle A is human face versus student document; tone shifts from optimistic to trustworthy.

### Angle C — The OET Healthcare Professional: One Place, Score and Visa

**Hypothesis:** Working healthcare professionals (nurses, doctors) preparing for OET as the route to UK or Australia work migration are a sharply self-selecting, high-intent audience who need the exact combination Verbis Edu provides — OET coaching and overseas work placement guidance — and who will self-identify immediately from an identity-first trigger in a Meta feed.

**Why:** The research confirms Verbis lists OET coaching alongside overseas work guidance. The India-to-UK/Australia nursing pipeline is active. Crucially, this audience already knows what OET is; the ad does not need to educate. The trigger — "Are you a nurse aiming to work in the UK or Australia?" — is one of the cleanest possible self-selectors available: job title as identity, not a soft demographic. Unlike the student angles, this audience converts year-round, making it a genuine portfolio hedge that teaches something independent of post-exam seasonality. If this angle wins, the strategic implication is significant: Verbis should build a dedicated OET-to-work-abroad service line and market it explicitly.

**Visual lane:** Functional and direct. A Verbis OET result or certificate in the foreground — a real document from the available student-results asset library — with training context visible behind it (centre, study materials). The professional identity of the viewer does the emotional work; the ad's job is to confirm capability. Distinct from Angles A and B in tone: those have human warmth; this has professional directness.

## 5. The Honest Flag

**Precondition — Pixel and tracking (must resolve before any spend):**
There is no Meta pixel on the /apply form and no CPL tracking history. Both must be in place before a single rupee is committed. Without them the test cannot be read, and the founder will have no baseline for any future test either. If running Meta lead generation forms (recommended below), conversion tracking is native to Meta and does not require a site pixel — but the choice of form type must be made before launch.

**Precondition — No CPL target confirmed:**
The founder has not stated a per-student economic value for the study-abroad service or a tolerable cost-per-lead. I am proceeding on the assumption that this service is high-value per student and that a CPL of roughly ₹800–1,500 would be commercially workable — but this is a rough category estimate, not the founder's number, and it must be confirmed. If it is wrong, the test will be judged against the wrong benchmark, and a good result may be killed or a bad one kept.

**Non-creative ceiling — placement proof gap:**
The study-abroad placement claims on the site are self-stated. There is no third-party review base for placement or visa outcomes the way there is for coaching. In a category where scam-awareness content is pervasive, a sceptical parent or student who searches before submitting will find strong coaching proof and weak placement proof. No creative fix addresses this. The fix is: collect placed students' permission to photograph their admit letters and visa approvals, publish them as documented case studies, and ask placed students explicitly for a Google review that names the outcome. This cannot be ready before launch — it must run in parallel. Expect conversion rates to improve meaningfully once visible placement proof exists. The test will still teach which audience self-selects and clicks; it will under-report true conversion potential until this proof exists.

**Budget note — use Meta lead generation forms:**
₹80,000 / month across three ad sets is approximately ₹2,650 / day total — a learning budget, not a scaling budget. At typical India education CPL rates (rough estimate: ₹500–1,000), this budget is unlikely to clear Meta's learning-phase threshold of approximately 50 conversion events per week per ad set. Recommendation: run all three angles as **Meta lead generation form ads** rather than click-to-website ads. Lead gen forms in India education typically produce lower CPL than landing-page traffic, keep the lead inside Meta's native experience (reducing drop-off), and do not require a fully seasoned pixel. Judge the six-week test on CPL and booked-call rate per angle, not on week-one volume.

**Timing — launch immediately:**
Angle A expires. The post-exam urgency window for Class 12 students closes by late July 2026 at the latest. Angles B and C are not calendar-constrained, but there is no reason to delay them either. The test window is now through end of June; read results at six weeks.
