**Hero-product JTBD (the whey, keyed to reference image):**

### 2d. Decision Criteria (synthesized from 2a + 2b)

Based on both brand-specific and category-level voice:

1. **Ingredient honesty / transparency** — buyers explicitly verify the label and prefer fewer, recognizable ingredients. "Artificial" is a disqualifier.
2. **Digestive tolerability** — buyers cite bloating and gas as reasons to *avoid* entire category or switch brands. No bloating is a major decision-sway.
3. **Taste relative to expectations** — buyers who want "dessert-like" and buyers who want "clean-tasting" are different segments. Taste is a *retention* friction, not a *trial* friction if the product is framed correctly upfront.
4. **Price per serving / per kg** — Indian market is price-conscious. Buyers compare ₹-per-gram across brands. Premium pricing is accepted if justified by ingredient quality or perceived value.
5. **Mixability / usability** — can it be made with just water or basic milk? Or does it need a blender? Buyers testing on-the-go usability.

### 2e. Alternatives Landscape

Indian whey protein buyers compare across:

- **Domestic brands:** MuscleBlaze Biozyme (₹3,300–3,400/kg, most common), OZiva (₹3,085/kg for women's blend), budget domestic brands (₹1,500–2,500/kg per Protein India 2026 source)
- **Imported brands:** Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard, MyProtein (e.g., MyProtein mentioned in reviews), Dymatize (less prominent in Indian searches but present)
- **Do-nothing:** Skip powder, get protein from food. Category-level data confirms many Indians avoid protein powder due to digestive fear.

**TWTF's competitive position:** Premium domestic, clean-label positioning, slightly higher price than MuscleBlaze but comparable to or lower than imported ON Gold Standard (which is ₹4,500–6,500/kg per market context, though no 2026 India listing confirmed).

### 2f. Category-Level Objections

From the category research, the objections the strategist needs to clear:

1. **"All whey makes me bloated."** — This is a *category* belief, not a brand belief. TWTF has evidence to counter this (digestive comfort reviews), but cold traffic will not know it. Positioning and messaging must surface this counter-claim early.
2. **"Protein powder tastes like chemicals."** — TWTF's positioning as "clean" and "natural-tasting" directly addresses this, but for buyers used to Godrej or MuscleBlaze's sweeter formulas, it may still read as "bland."
3. **"Why pay premium when budget brands work?"** — Value justification. TWTF's claim ("clean, no artificial stuff") is supported by reviews but must be articulated in the ad, not assumed.
4. **"I don't know which brand to trust."** — Transparency and certification signals matter. TWTF's "Trustified" badge (visible on reference image) and detailed label are assets but not default-known to cold traffic.
