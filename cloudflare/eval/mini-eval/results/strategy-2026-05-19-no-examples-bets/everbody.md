# The Bet — Ever/Body NYC
**Prepared:** 2026-05-19

---

## 1. The Memo

Ever/Body has something the NYC medspa market almost never has, and is not currently advertising: a genuinely low-pressure, welcoming experience. Across hundreds of reviews, first-time patients volunteer — unprompted — that no one pushed them into anything, that consultations didn't feel salesy, that they finally found a place that didn't make them feel judged. In a category where hard upselling and fear of looking "overdone" are the two biggest reasons curious people stay stuck and never book, that proof is rare and commercially significant.

The problem is that your current ads look like everyone else's — before/afters and a percentage-off offer. A cold stranger scrolling Instagram cannot distinguish you from Skinney running "10 units free" or any other injector running a deal. You are marketing the category when you should be marketing the reason to choose *you*. The "world-class experts / gold standard" positioning on the website competes directly with SkinSpirit — a 20-year-old, surgeon-founded national chain — on their strongest ground, and loses. The story the brand can actually win on is sitting in the reviews.

The test below runs three angles, each a different expression of the same underlying truth: this is the safe place to take your first step. The angles test whether the conversion driver is the *experience* (you won't be pressured), the *price conversation* (you can afford to find out), or the *outcome* (you won't look done). That tells us which framing of the trust story moves cold NYC strangers to book a first appointment — because it is the one story that is both genuinely true and uncontested in the market.

One structural note before spending anything: at $8,000/month, the Meta conversion event should be a **consultation-request form submission**, not a completed booking. Completed bookings won't generate enough weekly volume for Meta's algorithm to learn across three ad sets at this budget. Lead forms will. The clinic team converts leads to bookings through its existing follow-up process — that part of the funnel already works.

---

## 2. Conversion Event

A submitted consultation-request form (a "lead") — the Meta-optimised event. The downstream business goal is a first booked appointment; the optimisation event is the form fill, because booking volume is insufficient at this budget for clean learning. The clinic's follow-up team closes the gap between form and chair.

---

## 3. The Blocker

A cold, qualified prospect encountering Ever/Body's current ads sees something indistinguishable from any other NYC medspa running before/afters and a discount. The category's two biggest consumer fears — being pressured into treatments you don't need, and leaving looking visibly "done" — go completely unaddressed. The brand has credible, organic, unprompted proof against both fears and is not using it. The blocker is the message, not the product.

---

## 4. The Angles

The 20% off first-visit offer is a standing new-patient promotion and should appear consistently across all three cells as a supporting CTA detail — *not* as the headline. Keeping it constant means the offer is not the variable being tested; the message framing is. This is deliberate: we need to know whether the brand story alone can move cold traffic, not whether a discount does.

---

### Angle A — The No-Pressure Room

**Hypothesis:** The primary conversion blocker for qualified first-timers is anxiety about being sold to. An ad that explicitly names and defuses that fear — "the medspa where no one pushes you into anything" — will outperform the current discount-led format with this audience.

**Why (anchored in evidence):** The single most consistent unprompted theme in Ever/Body's reviews across all NYC locations is that the experience is low-pressure and not salesy — reviewers use specific language like "never pressure to purchase" and "did not feel salesly" without being asked. This is not a generic brand claim; it is what first-time patients document after the fact. No competitor runs this message; the market clusters around authority credentials and deals. The category's own well-documented reputation for hard upselling means this is a point of real differentiation, not positioning inflation.

**Visual lane:** Warm, candid clinic photography — real providers with patients, relaxed and unhurried. Soft lighting in the contemporary space. The mood should read as *this is not the intimidating dermatologist's office* — belonging, not aspiration. No transformation imagery in this cell. The human interaction in the room is the product being shown.

---

### Angle B — What's It Actually Going to Cost?

**Hypothesis:** Pricing anxiety is a distinct pre-booking barrier — curious people don't book because they don't know if they can afford it and don't want to walk into a consultation unable to say yes. An ad that opens the cost conversation honestly ("here's roughly what to expect; we'll tell you exactly at your visit") will convert better than one leading with a percentage discount, by removing the uncertainty that stalls booking intent.

**Why (anchored in evidence):** Price is the single most common negative review theme — "they overcharge," "the price is extremely high." This signals that cost is on prospects' minds before they arrive as much as after. The website lists no prices; consultations are where cost is set. The brand's founding identity was explicitly built around price transparency — that DNA is retrievable. A hook that says "let's be straight about what this costs" is both true to the brand's origin and addresses a documented barrier. It is a distinct entry point from a discount ("here's a real number to reason from") rather than a vague deal.

**Visual lane:** Clean and text-prominent, anchored by a real provider photo or short direct-to-camera video clip. A calm, no-fluff tone — the provider speaks plainly, without jargon, about what to expect. No clinical imagery. The provider's real face carries the credibility; the copy carries the honesty. The feeling is: *a straight answer from someone who knows.*

**Precondition (see Flag 1 below):** This angle makes a promise the current destination may not keep. Before running Angle B, the consultation landing page needs one sentence of honest pricing context — e.g. "most first visits start at [X]; your exact plan is set at your consultation." Without that, the ad generates curiosity the site immediately disappoints.

---

### Angle C — You'll Look Like You

**Hypothesis:** Fear of looking "overdone" or unnatural is a distinct barrier from pressure anxiety — some prospects stay away because they've seen bad work on others, not because of sales anxiety. An ad leading with subtle, natural-looking results ("refreshed, not done") will convert this segment better than either experience or cost messaging.

**Why (anchored in evidence):** Fear of unnatural results is a widely-voiced category concern, and Ever/Body's real results — as documented across its reviews and available photography — skew natural-looking. The visual assets to prove this exist. Competitors run before/after ads too, but the standard category execution is dramatic transformation. A before/after that shows subtle, flattering improvement ("you look like yourself, just rested") is a different message even in the same format. This is the most conventional angle of the three — worth one slot because if it outperforms the trust-led angles, we've learned the audience is more outcome-driven than experience-driven.

**Visual lane:** Real before/after photography — real patients, subtle and natural transformation. The creative brief: the "after" must not look obviously "done." Side-by-side or a soft reveal treatment, warm colour tone, not clinical whites. The copy framing — "Results that look like you" — is the differentiator from the typical dramatic B/A the rest of the category runs. This is what makes the cell distinct from competitors, not the format.

---

## 5. The Honest Flags

**Flag 1 — Landing page gap (precondition for Angle B, worth fixing for all three):**
The pricing-transparency hook in Angle B will frustrate users who click through and find no pricing guidance on the destination page — potentially damaging the brand's honesty positioning rather than building it. Fix: add a single line of honest pricing context to the consultation landing page before launching. Something like: "Most first visits start at $[X]; your exact treatment plan and cost are set at your consultation — no obligation." This is a fast copy change and it protects both Angle B's conversion rate and the brand's credibility for all traffic.

**Flag 2 — Optimise for leads, not bookings:**
The conversion event Must be a form fill / consultation request, not a completed booking. At $8,000/month across three ad sets, completed bookings will not reach the volume Meta needs to exit its learning phase (~50 conversion events per week per ad set). Optimising for an event that rarely fires produces noise, not signal. A lead form generates enough volume for the algorithm to learn and for results to be comparable across angles. This is the correct structure for this budget — not a compromise.

**Flag 3 — Unit economics are unknown:**
The founder did not provide average first-visit ticket value or patient LTV. Without these, a precise viable CPL cannot be confirmed. Working from category context only (not a founder-confirmed figure — treat this as an estimate), a CPL under $50–70 would likely sustain healthy economics for a NYC cosmetic dermatology first visit; if early data shows CPL consistently above $100, revisit budget allocation and audience targeting before the full month is spent. The founder should share a first-visit ticket average before launch so a clear success threshold can be set. Running without one means judging the test on a number nobody agreed on in advance.

**Flag 4 — Timing note:**
Mid-May is a favourable window — the spring peak for cosmetic dermatology demand in NYC (pre-summer). Beginning now gives a clean 6–8 week read before summer travel patterns begin to fragment attention in mid-July. Start the test in the next two weeks to use the seasonal tailwind.
