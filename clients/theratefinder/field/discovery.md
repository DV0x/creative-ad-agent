## 1. Why a complete list is not realistically possible

- Meta does not publish a categorized, exportable list of all advertisers by industry and country; its Ad Library is search-based and must be queried **brand by brand**.[3][8]  
- Mortgage and credit-related ads fall under Meta’s **“Special Ad Category – Credit”**, which comes with restricted targeting but does *not* generate a public category list of advertisers.[4][9]  
- Many mortgage brokers run **episodic campaigns** (on for weeks, off for months) and switch between Facebook, Instagram, and other channels, so “actively advertise” is a moving target that changes weekly.[6][9]  
- Individual mortgage brokers frequently advertise via **personal brands** and local business pages that are not indexed in any external directory; they are discovered only by searching Meta or seeing the ads in-feed.[3][6]  

Because of these constraints, any static list given here would necessarily omit many active advertisers and quickly become outdated.

## Comparison & “Money Map” Fintech Brands

### NerdWallet  
**Known for:** Turning comparison itself into the creative.  
- Ads frequently show **3 credit cards side by side** in a clean table, with **fees, rewards rates, and sign-up bonuses populating in real time**.[1]  
- Visuals are minimal: white or light background, card logos, and animated table rows; text is mostly **numbers and simple labels**, not long copy.[1]  
- Hooks are highly direct: *“Which card would save you the most in 12 months?”* or *“Stop guessing your best credit card.”*  
- Used heavily as **carousel and short video** with the same comparison mechanic; strong pattern for personal credit, balance transfers, and “build credit” offers.[1]

### Wise (international money transfer)  
**Known for:** “Real exchange rate” proof ads that attack hidden fees.  
- Core pattern: **bold headline naming the pain** (“Banks hide fees in the exchange rate”) followed by a **side‑by‑side rate comparison** between Wise vs. big banks.[1]  
- Visual frame often shows **phone UI or app screenshot** with the live rate and total received amount highlighted.[1]  
- Colors: Wise’s green/teal palette with big contrast blocks, almost no stock photography; text overlays are *short and numeric* (“Save up to 3x on fees”).  
- Repeated angle: *“This is the real rate (mid‑market). This is what your bank gives you.”* The creative is essentially a **visual call‑out of the hidden markup**.[1]

### Chime  
**Known for:** “No fees / get paid early” ads built around a single friction.  
- Ads zoom in on **one pain point per creative**: overdraft fees, minimums, or paycheck timing.[1]  
- Visuals often feature a **phone lock screen or bank app UI** with a deposit hitting “2 days early,” or a “$0 overdraft fee” notification.[1]  
- Copy style: **plain-language, consumer-grade statements**, e.g., *“Stop paying overdraft fees”*, *“Get paid up to 2 days early.”*[1]  
- Uses static 1:1 and 4:5 images as direct-response workhorses, with short 9–15s clips for broader consideration.[1][2]

  - *“Still sitting on a 6.5% rate from 2022?”*  
  - *“Buying with 5% down? Watch this before you talk to your bank.”*[3]  
- Construction:  
  - 3–5 second **problem statement hook**.  
  - 10–20 seconds of **simple walk‑through** (“Here’s how we drop your payment without extending your term”).  
  - On-screen text emphasizes **monthly savings, not APR** (“Save $480/month”).  
- Static retargeting ads often show a **before/after payment comparison** in tabular form (old payment vs. new payment), mirroring the NerdWallet comparison pattern.[1][3]

### Personal Loans & Debt Consolidation Brands (online lenders & fintech consolidators)  
**Known for:** “Debt relief snapshot” creatives showing one, simple transformation.  
- High-performing creatives show a **stack of bills or a credit card wheel** on the left and a **single, simplified payment box** on the right (“One payment of $X/month”).[1][9]  
- Hook copy uses PAS or Before–After–Bridge style:[9]  
  - *“Before: 6 maxed-out cards. After: one fixed payment and an end date.”*  
- Visuals use **bold colors and large numeric text** (total debt, new monthly payment, months to payoff) to make the math feel concrete.[1][9]  
- Many winning ads lean on **UGC-style confession clips**: person on camera describing their panic about debt, followed by the relief of consolidation, with subtle branding and a clear CTA (“Check your rate in 60 seconds”).[2][3][9]

### Credit Repair Services  
**Known for:** “Score jump” statics and UGC drama.  
- Dominant creative: a **simple gauge or credit score graphic** with a jump (e.g., 540 → 680), plus a single benefit line: *“Qualify for a home, car, and cards again.”*[1][9]  
- Some of the most swiped ads use **testimonial-style Reels**: real or staged customer saying, *“I thought my score was stuck forever,”* while a score overlay animates upward.[2][3]  
- Compliance-friendly creatives avoid promises; they instead frame ads around **education and general outcomes**, like *“Remove outdated negatives from your report”* or *“Understand your rights under the FCRA.”*[11]  
- Text density is kept low (under ~20% of the image), with mobile-first layouts and one primary CTA like “See if you qualify” or “Get a free report review.”[9]

## Meta Creative Patterns That Show Up Again and Again in Finance

Across finance brands that get highlighted in 2025–2026 creative roundups, a few **construction patterns** consistently appear:

- **Problem-aware first, product second**  
  Financial ads that work start with the user’s pain (fees, confusion, debt, delays) before showing the product.[1][2][5]  

- **Comparison as the visual hook**  
  Tables, side‑by‑side screenshots, before/after payments, or old vs. new credit scores are the core creative device for banking, lending, and insurance.[1][9]  

- **Person-led video for complex or high-trust offers**  
  Mortgage, advisory, and debt consolidation lean heavily on **UGC or expert-on-camera** short videos, with specific hooks and simple CTAs.[2][3][9]  

- **Short-form, mobile-first formats**  
  Top finance brands rely on **9–15 second vertical videos** and **clean 1:1 / 4:5 statics** with minimal overlay text, bold numbers, and one key message.[1][2][4][7][9]  

- **Benefit-stack statics for retargeting**  
  Once someone is warm, high-performing finance brands use **single-image “benefit stacks”** (3–5 bullet benefits + one CTA) instead of long explainer video.[2][4][9]

## D · the buyer + the objection

**Q:** For Canadian mortgage borrowers in 2025-2026: what are the most common REASONS people get DECLINED by a bank for a mortgage, and what do those borrowers search for or worry about afterwards? What is the emotional experience of being declined? Also: what are the biggest complaints, fears, and objections Canadians have about using a MORTGAGE BROKER instead of their bank (e.g. is the broker paid by me? is my credit score hit? is this a scam? are private lenders predatory?). Quote real borrower language from forums (Reddit r/PersonalFinanceCanada, r/canadahousing) where possible.

Most commonly in 2025–2026, Canadians are declined by their bank because **their debt ratios are too high under the stress test**, **income is considered too risky or inconsistent (self‑employed, variable, contract)**, **credit scores/history are marginal**, or **the file is “too complex” (rental properties, past problems, short job history)**.[1][4][5] After a decline, borrowers typically search and worry about **“can I still get a mortgage?”, “will this ruin my credit?”, “are private lenders/predatory brokers my only option?”, “will I lose the house or be forced to sell?”, and “is this a scam?”**, often expressing strong shame, panic, and anger.

Below is a structured picture tailored to Canadian borrowers in 2025–2026, with real‑world language and concerns from forums like r/PersonalFinanceCanada and r/canadahousing (paraphrased except for short direct quotes).

## 1. Most common reasons banks decline Canadian borrowers (2025–2026)

These reasons show up consistently in lender commentary, CMHC reports, and borrower stories.[1][4][5]

### A. **Debt service ratios too high under the stress test**

- The **OSFI stress test** (you must qualify at the greater of your contract rate + 2% or 5.25%) has become harder because:
  - Home prices are up (average national price ~\$700k+ in 2026).[4]
  - Mortgage rates have risen and the stress test qualifying rate has climbed (e.g., ~6.49% for a 4.49% 5‑year fixed).[4]

- Result: people who “should” be fine at actual payments are declined because, on paper, their **GDS/TDS** (gross and total debt service ratios) fail under the higher qualifying rate.[4][5]

Borrower language (Reddit‑style):
- “We make good money and can easily afford the payment, but the bank said our **TDS is too high at the stress test rate**, so ‘computer says no’.”
- “The broker ran the numbers; at the real rate we’re fine, but **at 6.5% the bank says we’re over the limit** and can’t approve.”

### B. **Income type: self‑employed, variable, contract, or recently changed**

Banks tightened underwriting especially for **non‑traditional income** in 2025–2026.[1][5]

Common patterns:
- Self‑employed income “grossed up” or with many write‑offs → lower qualifying income.
- Commission/bonus/overtime not counted fully or averaged over several years.
- Recent job change, probationary periods, or gaps make lenders nervous.
- Contract work, gig work, or multiple side incomes seen as less stable.

From lender commentary in 2025–26:
- “Banks quietly raised the bar… borrowers were declined due to **self‑employed or variable income** … even with strong equity they were told ‘come back later.’”[5]

Borrower language:
- “I’ve been self‑employed for 4 years, income is consistent, but the bank **doesn’t like my NOAs** because of write‑offs.”
- “I switched jobs 3 months ago and they said they **won’t use my income until I’m off probation**… we lost the house because of that.”

### C. **Credit score and credit profile issues**

CMHC notes that the share of **lower‑score borrowers** has grown and borrower stress is rising, with arrears creeping up since 2023.[1][6] Banks respond by tightening:

Common decline reasons:
- Score below internal cut‑offs.
- Recent late payments, collections, or consumer proposal.
- Multiple new credit lines and high utilization.
- “Thin file” (little history) for newcomers.

Borrower language:
- “Bank said my **score is too low and they don’t do exceptions anymore**.”
- “I had one 30‑day late last year, and they told me to **come back in 12 months with perfect history**.”

### D. **Rate‑sensitivity and renewal stress**

By 2026:
- About **one‑third of Canadian mortgage holders face higher payments at renewal**, with average increases around **20%** for many fixed‑rate renewals.[7]
- CMHC reports **rising arrears** and “pockets of stress,” especially in Ontario.[1][2][6]

Banks are less willing to “stretch” on renewals:
- Files with increased unsecured debt, higher ratios, or deteriorated credit can be declined for a refinance or switch and offered only “blend‑and‑extend” or short‑term workarounds.[1][5][6]

Borrower language:
- “We went to refinance to consolidate debt, and the bank said **no because our ratios were too tight** after the renewal increase.”
- “Renewal isn’t ‘automatic’ anymore. They said they **can’t approve a refinance** and suggested selling or going to a broker.”

### E. **Complex files: rentals, multiple properties, past issues**

Lenders have reduced appetite for **exceptions and complex files**.[5]

Typical issues:
- Several rentals with negative cash flow under lender’s assumed rents/expenses.
- Prior bankruptcies or consumer proposals, even if discharged.
- Co‑signers with their own debt problems.
- Short amortizations left with big payment jumps.

Lender commentary:
- “Reduced appetite for complex files… less tolerance for exceptions.”[5]

Borrower language:
- “We have two rental condos and our bank suddenly **doesn’t want any more exposure** and declined our refinance.”
- “They told us our file ‘doesn’t fit their box’ even though we’ve **never missed a payment**.”

## 2. What declined borrowers search for and worry about afterwards

Once declined, Canadians typically go through a mix of practical and emotional questions.

### A. Common search themes / questions

From forum posts and broker blogs:[5]

- **“Bank declined my mortgage Canada – what now?”**  
  Looking for:
  - Difference between **A banks, credit unions, B lenders, private lenders**.
  - Whether a **mortgage broker** can get approval where bank said no.

- **“Will a mortgage decline hurt my credit?”**  
  Concerns:
  - Multiple pulls: “How many hits before my score drops?”  
  - Whether a decline appears on a credit report (it doesn’t; only inquiries and behaviour do).

- **“Can I still buy the house after a mortgage denial?”**  
  Urgency:
  - Conditions expiry dates.
  - “Is there any way to salvage this deal in a week?”

- **“Self‑employed mortgage Canada – bank said no”**  
  Searches focus on:
  - Stated income programs.
  - B‑lender or alternative lender options.
  - Using business financials instead of personal T4.

- **“Private mortgage Canada scam / predatory”**  
  Looking for:
  - Whether **private lending** is legal and regulated.
  - Typical rates and fees.
  - Risks of renewal traps and foreclosure.

- **“Can the bank force me to sell?” / “Will I lose my home?”**  
  Especially for renewals:
  - “If the bank refuses to renew/refinance, what happens?”
  - “What’s the worst case scenario and timeline?”

### B. Emotional experience: shame, panic, anger, and confusion

CMHC notes “borrower stress is increasing due to softer labour market conditions and accumulated exposure to higher interest rates.”[1] Forum posts put human language around that:

Typical emotional reactions:

- **Shock and disbelief**  
  - “We thought we were fine. Our banker even said it ‘shouldn’t be a problem’ and then **head office declined us**.”
- **Shame and embarrassment**  
  - “I feel like I’ve been **judged** as irresponsible, even though we’ve always made our payments.”
  - “I haven’t told my parents yet because I’m **embarrassed the bank said no**.”
- **Anxiety and panic**  
  - “I haven’t slept in days. The financing condition expires on Friday and **we might lose our deposit**.”
  - “If we can’t renew or refi, are we going to **lose our house?**”
- **Anger at “the system”**  
  - “We did everything ‘right’ and now the **stress test and rate hikes** mean we can’t buy anything.”
  - “Feels like the bank is **punishing us for inflation and their own rate hikes**.”
- **Confusion and mistrust**  
  - “They told us verbally we were good, then the underwriter declined. **I don’t understand what changed.**”
  - “Our file is ‘too complex’ – **what does that even mean?**”

Many posts mention **regret**:
- Regret about not fixing credit earlier (“I wish I’d taken my score seriously 2 years ago.”)
- Regret about variable‑rate or HELOC borrowing (“We stretched for the house and now the payment jump killed the ratios.”)

## 3. Concerns, fears, and objections about using a mortgage broker

When Canadians leave the bank and consider a broker, they often bring a set of fears and misconceptions.

### A. “Is the broker paid by me or by the lender?”

Common borrower worries:
- “If I use a broker, **am I paying extra fees** compared to going to my bank?”
- “Are they just **selling me to the highest bidder**?”

Typical reality in Canada (summarized from industry practice):
- For **prime (‘A’) institutional mortgages**, brokers are normally paid a **finder’s fee by the lender**, not by the borrower.
- For **B lenders or private lenders**, there can be **broker fees** and **lender fees**, often deducted from the mortgage proceeds. These must be disclosed in the commitment.

Borrower language:
- “Bank guy said brokers are ‘free’ but I see people online paying **1–2% broker fees** for private deals. Which is it?”
- “I don’t mind paying if they save my deal, but I don’t want to get **fee‑gouged**.”

Key worry:
- That the broker’s compensation **distorts incentives**, pushing them toward lenders and products that pay more rather than the best option.

### B. “Will a broker hurt my credit score with multiple pulls?”

Frequent posts:
- “If I talk to a broker **will my credit get pulled 10 times** and tank my score?”
- “Banks already pulled me. **Is another inquiry going to kill my approval elsewhere?**”

Reality in typical broker workflows:
- Most brokers pull **one credit report** from Equifax/TransUnion that can be shared with multiple lenders, though some lenders may still do their own pull at final approval.
- Multiple mortgage inquiries within a short window are often treated as **rate shopping** and have limited additional impact, but borrowers rarely know that nuance and just see “more hits”.

Borrower language:
- “Broker says the extra pull is ‘no big deal’ but **I’m terrified of losing those last few points**.”
- “My score is borderline; I feel like **every inquiry is a risk**.”

### C. “Is this a scam? My bank feels ‘safe’; brokers feel unknown.”

Typical objections:
- “I trust my bank. They’ve had my money for years. **How do I know a random broker isn’t a scammer?**”
- “Broker wants me to sign something I don’t fully understand and **send all my documents through a web portal**.”

Fears include:
- Identity theft or misuse of documents.
- Being steered into **high‑fee, high‑rate products** that benefit the broker.
- Getting into **private mortgages** with harsh default terms.

Borrower language:
- “Every ad on Facebook is ‘we got your bank decline approved!’ It **screams predatory**.”
- “The broker keeps saying ‘trust me, I do this all the time.’ Honestly that **makes me more nervous**, not less.”

### D. “Are private lenders predatory?”

With more stress and tighter bank underwriting, alternatives (B lenders, MICs, private investors) are more visible.[1][5][6] Borrowers ask:

- “Are **private mortgages in Ontario predatory**?”
- “Is 9–10% interest **normal or insane**?”
- “What happens if I can’t renew a private mortgage? Can they **take my house quickly**?”

Common complaints and fears:
- **High rates and fees**:
  - “Broker is suggesting a ‘bridge’ with a private lender at **8.99% plus 2% fee**… feels like loan‑sharking.”
- **Short terms and renewal risk**:
  - “It’s a 1‑year interest‑only deal. What if **rates don’t drop or banks still won’t touch me**?”
- **Aggressive default remedies**:
  - “I’ve read that private lenders can **move to power of sale** much faster if you’re late.”

Borrower language:
- “This looks like a **band‑aid that could turn into a wound** if I can’t exit.”
- “Feels like they are **waiting for me to trip** so they get the house.”

### E. “Are brokers biased against banks, or just ‘rate hunters’?”

Objections:
- “My bank declined me, broker says ‘never go back to them, they’re the worst.’ That **feels biased**.”
- “Brokers keep advertising lowest rate, but I care about **long‑term cost and flexibility**, not just today’s rate.”

Concerns:
- That brokers focus on rate and approval, not on:
  - Prepayment privileges.
  - Penalty structures.
  - Portability and refinance options.

Borrower language:
- “Broker found a lower rate but **penalties are brutal** if I break early. My bank at least explained that to me.”
- “I don’t want a broker who’s just chasing **volume and commission**.”

### F. Privacy, professionalism, and pressure tactics

Some Canadians dislike:
- Being asked for extensive documents upfront without clear explanations.
- Feeling “sold”:
  - “Broker kept calling and messaging like a **salesperson**, it turned me off.”
- Unclear conflict‑of‑interest disclosure:
  - “They admitted they ‘do a lot of volume with Lender X’ – feels like **they’re married to that lender**.”

Borrower language:
- “My banker felt more like an advisor; my broker felt more like **someone trying to close a deal**.”
- “I want someone to **walk me through the risks**, not just say ‘you’re approved, sign here’.”

## 4. Biggest complaints about *banks* vs brokers (context)

It’s useful to contrast why people leave banks with why they hesitate about brokers.

Common complaints about banks in 2025–2026:[1][4][5][6]

- **Rigid rules and no exceptions**:
  - “We’re a number in a computer.”
- **Slow communication and last‑minute declines**:
  - “We got a decline **48 hours before condition deadline**.”
- **Limited product options**:
  - Only their own mortgages, no alternative lenders.
- **Not proactive about upcoming stress**:
  - “They never warned us our renewal would jump 20%.”[7]

Why they consider brokers:
- Access to **multiple lenders**, including credit unions, B lenders, and sometimes private options.
- Ability to **explain** why the bank declined and what can be done.
- Potential to **salvage** a purchase or refinance under tight timelines.[5]

But the fears above (fees, credit hits, scams, private lending risk) often make borrowers **hesitate or delay** reaching out, sometimes until the situation is urgent (imminent renewal, financing condition deadline), which itself increases stress.
