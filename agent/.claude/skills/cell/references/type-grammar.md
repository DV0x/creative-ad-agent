# The Type Grammar — the maker's handwriting, not a font you like

The maker has already been named (`layer-stack.md`, step 3). This document is what you consult next, when the staging asks one question: **so what do this maker's words look like?**

Type is not picked. **Type is the maker's handwriting** — the lettering that maker would *actually* have produced, never a typeface you reach for because it's tasteful. A real Amazon reviewer didn't set their words in a brand font; the app did. A founder scrawling on a whiteboard didn't open a type foundry. Name the maker, and the type is already decided — this grammar just tells you what each kind of lettering *signals*, so you can read it back off the maker instead of guessing.

> **The lettering is downstream of the maker. One maker, one hand.** Two type voices in a frame (a designed brand headline over a real phone photo) read as two images stapled together — the exact v6/v7 failure. The maker disciplines the type the same way it disciplines the copy.

**The honest ceiling — what the renderer can actually act on.** You cannot hand the image model a typeface name; "set in Söhne" means nothing to it. What it *can* render is a **vernacular** plus a few knobs: **weight** (light → black), **case** (lowercase / Title / UPPER), **width** (condensed → extended), **contrast** (even strokes vs. thick-thin), and a real reference to imitate ("the iOS system font," "Impact-style meme caps," "a handwritten marker," "a premium DTC wordmark"). And it renders text *cleanly* when given the **exact words and where they sit** — so the job here is the *kind* of lettering, never the glyphs. Every entry below resolves to a vernacular + knobs, not a font.

**This is a place to dig, not a closed menu.** The entries are common hands, not the only ones — read the maker first and resolve to whatever lettering *that* maker truly used.

---

## How to read an entry

Each entry has four parts:

- **Signal** — what this lettering says to a viewer in the first half-second
- **When earned** — the maker (or situation) that makes it the honest call
- **Resolve to** — the *real, nameable* convention to render (the part the model won't reach for on its own)
- **The fake twin** — the generic version that wears the same clothes, and why it's forbidden

---

## Entries

The hands run roughly from the most *found* (a real screen, a real pen), through the *borrowed* ones that carry someone else's authority (a stamp, a magazine), to the most *designed* (a brand's own type). You don't shop this list — you name the maker (`layer-stack.md`, step 3) and the maker hands you the lettering.

### System / native font — the platform's own UI type

- **Signal:** real, unedited, true. This text wasn't *set* by a designer — it was typed into an app and screenshotted. It reads as **collected, not made**, which is why it's the resting default for proof-shaped formats.
- **When earned:** the maker is a real digital artifact — a verified review, a DM, an IG story, a tweet, a Notes-app screenshot. Layer-stack step 3 lands you here whenever the line's provenance is "it already lives on a screen."
- **Resolve to:** the *actual* system or app font of the surface shown, with the **real chrome that font always travels with** — iOS San Francisco in a Messages bubble (timestamp, send arrow); Amazon's own type with its orange star row and "Verified Purchase · Amazon.in" tag; IG's story font laid over a photo; the X/Twitter UI around a tweet. Fidelity to a *real screen*, never "a clean modern sans."
- **The fake twin:** a designed "testimonial card" — a hand-picked brand font, kerned, on brand colors. The designer's hand is visible, so the viewer stops believing the quote is real. The worse cousin is **faked chrome** (invented stars, a made-up verified badge) — that's the honesty-gate violation from `layer-stack.md`, not just a style miss.

### Handwritten / personal — a human hand made this mark

- **Signal:** a person was here, not a brand. Personal, immediate, one-to-one — the intimacy of something written *for you*. The imperfection is what reads as honest.
- **When earned:** the maker leaves a mark by hand — a founder's note, a thank-you card tucked in the box, a doctor's scrawl, a whiteboard, a Post-it, or a hand marking up a photo (circling the detail that matters). Also when the claim is a personal promise or a correction made in the founder's own voice.
- **Resolve to:** a real instrument on a real surface — Sharpie on a whiteboard, ballpoint on a sticky note, marker circling a spot in a photo, an ink signature. The tells are an uneven baseline, real ink texture, a natural slant. Name the instrument and the surface, not "a handwriting font."
- **The fake twin:** a script *typeface* set evenly (Pacifico, a wedding-invite font) — perfect spacing and a dead-level baseline prove no hand made it. A brand *performing* "personal" reads as less honest than plain type, not more.

### Stamped / official — borrowed institutional authority

- **Signal:** verified, approved, on the record — the weight of an institution standing behind the claim.
- **When earned:** the maker is an institution or a process that leaves an official mark — a lab result, a certificate, a passport stamp, an inspection "PASSED," a notarization, a report card. Usually a proof / credibility format where a real body backs the claim.
- **Resolve to:** the real conventions of officialdom — a rubber-stamp impression (uneven ink, slightly rotated), an embossed or foil seal, a monospace / typewriter face for a "document," a certificate's formal serif and border, an ink date stamp. The slight wear of a *real* stamp is what sells it.
- **The fake twin:** inventing an official-looking seal or badge for a claim no institution actually backs. That is fabricated authority — the same fraud as faked review stars (the `layer-stack.md` honesty gate). The stamp must point to a real certification, or it doesn't get drawn.

### Set-on-a-grid / editorial — the magazine's considered hand

- **Signal:** curated, credible, journalism-grade. "An editor chose this" — borrowed trust from publishing.
- **When earned:** the maker is a publication or a designer working on a grid — a *real* feature ("as featured in," when it is true), a magazine-style brand, or a story / thought-leadership angle whose credibility move is "this reads like a respected outlet covered it."
- **Resolve to:** a real editorial vernacular — a high-contrast serif headline (the Vogue / NYT / Monocle family), a strict column grid, a drop cap, a kicker and standfirst, a byline, a pull quote set large, generous margins. The layout discipline of an actual spread.
- **The fake twin:** an "as seen in" logo bar of outlets that never covered the brand (already forbidden in `formats/testimonial.md`) — fabricated editorial credibility. Editorial polish with no real editorial substance behind it is costume. Real feature, or don't borrow the gown.

### Condensed-caps / loud — the poster shout

- **Signal:** volume. Urgency, news, a sale — the brand at full voice, stopping the scroll by sheer force of size.
- **When earned:** the made-object / poster branch (`layer-stack.md`, step 2) — when the take's voice *is* the delivery vehicle (the giant type IS the hook), a retail or offer moment (posters by birth), or the category wallpaper is quiet and constructed loudness is the contrast move. Loud must be *argued*; loud for no reason is just noise.
- **Resolve to:** a condensed or extra-condensed sans, black weight, ALL CAPS, tight leading, stacked to fill the frame edge to edge — the vernacular of a Swiss protest poster, a Nike OOH board, a tabloid front page, a boxing fight card. High ink coverage, hard alignment, no timid margins.
- **The fake twin:** "loud" that whispers — a medium-weight headline with polite air around it. If it isn't crowding the edges, it's a quiet ad wearing a big-type costume.

### Rounded / friendly — disarming and low-stakes

- **Signal:** approachable, warm, young, fun. Says "this is easy" — it lowers the buyer's guard.
- **When earned:** the maker is a brand whose positioning is friendly and accessible — a challenger DTC, a food or wellness brand, anything courting a younger buyer who is allergic to corporate. The claim is reassuring or playful, never high-stakes-serious.
- **Resolve to:** a rounded-terminal sans (the Circular / Poppins / rounded-DIN vernacular — Oatly's cousins, Headspace, Duolingo), medium-bold, often in a warm bright color with a little bounce. The soft corners are the whole tell.
- **The fake twin:** rounded-friendly on something high-stakes — a finance product, a luxury item, a medical claim. The type undercuts the claim and reads juvenile or untrustworthy. Friendliness has to match the actual stakes.

### Neutral-grotesk / premium — restraint as the flex

- **Signal:** expensive, calm, certain. "We don't need to shout." Quiet authority; taste itself is the argument.
- **When earned:** the explicit exception to native-default (`layer-stack.md`, step 3) — a brand whose *whole positioning is polish* (considered beauty, apparel, design-led tech or DTC). Earned only when the brand genuinely lives here; the argument is the product's quality, and the type has to embody it.
- **Resolve to:** a clean neutral grotesk (the Helvetica / Söhne / Inter vernacular), regular-to-medium weight, sentence case or lowercase, generous spacing, a tight restrained palette, real negative space — the air of an Aesop page or a Kinfolk spread. The space *is* the luxury signal.
- **The fake twin:** empty minimalism — a clean sans floating in white with nothing earned behind it. This is the "tasteful center" the whole pipeline fights. Premium type on a brand that isn't premium reads as generic, not luxe.

---

Type is one slice of the look. The larger vernacular — a real photo vs. a meme card vs. a constructed poster — is the **style grammar** (built next, same four-part shape); the type sits *inside* whatever the style lands on, in the same one-maker hand.
