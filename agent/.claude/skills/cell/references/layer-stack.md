# The Layer Stack — staging the picture, and where style actually comes from

A take's picture has two halves. **What is in it** — objects, action, composition — the drawability bar already governs. **How it exists as an image** — where the scene lives, who supposedly made the image, what the light and texture and type look like — is the *staging*, and it is the most stolen decision in the pipeline: leave any part of it silent and the renderer answers from the category average. That is how a sharp concept ships as wallpaper. The rule that fixes it:

> **No silent visual decisions — and no vague ones.** Every staging choice is made in the take, in words, with its reason — or the average makes it for you. **Vague is silent in disguise:** "shot on a phone," "a clean kitchen," "natural light" hand the specifics straight back to the renderer, exactly as if you'd said nothing. So the discipline is a **sweep** — before the render, walk *every* element (the camera and *which* device · the angle · the light's time, direction, and quality · the surface · everything in frame · the type · the CTA) and resolve each to a *specific, named, real* thing. Every pixel is a decision; the ones you leave unmade are made by the average.

Style is not a thing you pick. It is the *residue* of answering the questions below honestly. The model already knows every style that exists; what it lacks is a reason to reach for one. These questions are the reason.

---

## The derivation — ask in order

**1. Make the hook literally true.** The claim happened. What scene, or what artifact, now exists in the world because of it? "We framed our most boring review" → somewhere a real frame got hung by a real person. "We handle the paperwork to the end" → an 11pm WhatsApp reply, a stamped visa page, a checklist with 31 of 32 boxes ticked. A service with an invisible product still leaves artifacts — and artifacts have makers.

The **setting comes from the claim** — where it actually lives — never from the brand's existing product-photo style, **and never lifted from a prior render or a remembered example** (a setting that worked once is the *residue* of that brand's claim, not a template; reuse its surface on a new brief and the swap test fails by construction). Copying the brand's studio packshot background hands you a *void that argues nothing.* Split it cleanly: the **idea decides the setting** (where the scene happens), the **brand decides the treatment** (light quality, finish, palette). Take *both* from the brand's packshots and you get a packshot.

**And the setting has to be *plausible*, not just derived.** "Where the claim lives" is a hard test, not free association: it must be a real place where a real person would genuinely use, keep, or reach for **this** product — not merely any scene the claim's words could suggest. A place that is arbitrary, off-category, or faintly wrong for the product breaks belief even when every element is specific and un-generic (the picture reads as staged, not real). Before you commit a setting, ask the stranger's question — *"would someone actually be here, with this?"* — and if the honest answer is no, it is the wrong room, however vivid. Specific is necessary; **plausible** is also necessary.

**2. Found, or made? Two worlds, one rule.** The claim can be made literal in the **real world** — a found scene, a documentary moment, an artifact photographed where it lives. Or in an **invented world** — the claim's own verb made physical: *boost* becomes a finger flicking something slow into motion; *irresistible* becomes the painting reaching out of its frame. Impossible is fine; **undrawable is not** — a stranger can draw "turtle on a skateboard, a hand mid-flick." The metaphor is derived, never decorated: take the claim's central verb or noun and make it physically true.

And some pictures are not scenes at all but **made objects** — a poster, a chart, a side-by-side. The made branch opens for *derivable reasons only*: the take's voice is the brand at full volume (giant type IS the hook's delivery vehicle) · the claim is numeric or comparative (its native habitat is a graphic) · the category's wallpaper is quiet, so constructed loudness is the contrast move · it is an offer/retail moment (retail ads are posters by birth) · the claim cannot be photographed at all (a drawn image performs it).

**But a matched format's native rule outranks this branch.** When the take chose a format whose register is native/collected (a testimonial, a founder-POV), that register wins over the made-object reasons above — a testimonial earns belief by looking *collected*, not *designed*, so "the wallpaper is quiet" is no licence to turn it into a poster. Find the contrast *within* the format's register: a raw, real artifact stands out in a polished feed **more** than a designed poster does, and stays believable. The made-object branch is for angles built *freestyle* (no format matched), or for a format whose job *is* a made object.

**3. Who made this image? — the keystone.** Every image was made by someone, and that one fiction is what makes every *other* decision cohere: the light, the type, the framing, the texture all fall out of it. Name two makers and they fight — a designed brand headline floating over a real phone photo reads as two images stapled together. *If this image were real, who would have produced it, and why are they showing it to me?*

**Read the copy's provenance, not just its voice.** "A customer is speaking" is abstract — it lands on "a customer photo," still the average. Read where the line actually *comes from*: a verbatim Amazon.in verified-purchase review → the maker is **that review, as a real screenshot** — its stars, its Verified-Purchase tag, its reviewer, its date. The artifact the copy already lives in *is* the maker. The examples — a photographer · a designer on a grid · a meme page · an app's own UI · a hand with scissors and glue · an illustrator · an institution with a stamp · **a review screenshot · an Instagram story · a text thread** — only illustrate; the provenance governs.

**Default to native.** On today's feed a polished, brand-made image reads as *an ad* and gets skipped; a real artifact — a phone snap, a screenshot, a forwarded message — reads as *true*. Reach for the raw native maker first; a premium, designed maker is the exception you have to argue for (a brand whose whole positioning IS polish). **The voice and the provenance pick the maker; the maker picks everything else. One fiction per image.**

**4. Stand where the maker stood.** Once the maker is named, five questions answer themselves — a checklist that falls out, never five menus:

- who captured this, with what (studio strobe · phone flash · screenshot chrome · scanner)
- clean or worn (pristine · crooked · photocopied-thrice · compressed)
- what the words look like (set type on a grid · Impact · handwriting · UI font · stamp)
- did anyone mark it up (circle · highlight · scrawl · nothing)
- what is it sitting in (a frame · a feed · a wall · a phone screen · bare)

Two of these reads have their own dictionaries — which resolve them to a *specific, named* convention instead of the example menus above: the **whole look** (real photo · poster · borrowed · crafted · drawn) → `style-grammar.md`; the **lettering** → `type-grammar.md`. Both are read off this one maker, never picked.

Each maker carries its own honesty: a meme card must look like a real meme page made it; a screenshot needs real UI chrome; a "photo" needs a real photographer's texture — grain, true shadows, imperfection. **"Photorealistic" is never an instruction** — it is the *output* of naming a real photographer. The glossy, perfectly-lit, too-clean render is AI-gloss: the photoreal center, on the bank's forbid list.

**The maker disciplines the copy, too — not just the look.** Once you name the artifact, only the copy that could *honestly* appear in it survives. A single Amazon screenshot holds Amazon reviews — not one quote from the brand's website and one from Amazon in the same frame; that artifact never existed. If the chosen maker can't truthfully carry a line, the line moves (to the post copy) or the maker changes — you never fake the artifact to fit the copy. This is a check the *vague* look hides: a flat caption bar will hold any two quotes without complaint; a real screenshot exposes the lie. The specific maker is an honesty gate, not only an anti-generic one.

**5. Does the staging add?** Move the scene to a clean tabletop in soft light. Did the argument get weaker? If nothing broke, the staging isn't doing work yet — push further. The frame *on the office wall between real award certificates* extends the joke; the frame on a cream table only displays it.

**6. Is a default earned?** Landing on a plain product shot is legal exactly when the argument itself is "look at the product" — and the take's reason says so. An earned default is a decision; a landed-on default is the average. The written reason is the difference.

## The weld — borrowing what is already in the buyer's head

**Furniture** is anything THIS buyer recognizes in under one second with zero explanation — if it needs a caption, it isn't furniture. Common shelves (places to dig, never a closed list): their **language** (idioms, sayings, riddles — "which came first") · their **symbols** (turtle = slow) · their **images** (public-domain icons) · their **conventions** (trophies, report cards, gold seals, employee-of-the-month plaques) · their **screens** (feed chrome, notification badges, the offline dino — including the move where the ad breaks its own container) · their **shared moments** (the 3pm slump, the gym bro who won't stop). Furniture is **mined fresh per buyer, every run** — a Hyderabad hotel guest and a US whey buyer own different shelves. It is found, never invented, and never stored.

**The weld:** one *true thing from the room* × one *piece of the buyer's furniture*, where the flip, the answer, or the completion **is the claim itself**. The furniture buys the instant click; the truth does the selling. Grades, strictest first:

- **Welded to this brand's claim** — only this brand could run it. The bar.
- **Welded to the category's claim** — any rival could run it. That is not a craft failure to fix sideways; it is a flag that the angle's promise was category-generic. Route upstream.
- **Welded to nothing** — borrowed attention, a laugh that sells nothing. Dead.

Furniture is one road to a take, not a requirement — found scenes, proof devices, and demonstrations need none. And the borrowing has a legal floor: **cultural commons only** — idioms, riddles, public-domain art. A real person's face is a rights problem the founder inherits.

## Style — four questions, answered in the take with reasons

1. **What does the click need to land in two seconds?** Usually: an empty stage. Clutter kills the click — this question outranks the other three.
2. **How real must the impossible be?** Invented scenes render as *real photographs* by default — the realism IS the surprise (a cartoon turtle is expected to be strange). Deliberately artificial looks (collage, cartoon, meme-card) need a borrowed reason —
3. **What does this buyer's feed offer to borrow?** A style can itself be furniture: the meme-page look, the WhatsApp forward, newsprint, the retro poster — borrowed trust from a vernacular the buyer already lives in.
4. **What does the comp wallpaper forbid?** Whatever the category's ads all look like is the one look this ad must not have.

These four pressure-test the look the maker already named; resolve that look to a *specific, named* vernacular — with its real conventions and its forbidden twin — in `style-grammar.md`.

**The corridor every picture must fit:** vivid enough that the founder could not have made it themselves in Canva in ten minutes — *and* simple enough that the idea clicks in two seconds. The turtle passes both; over-produced cleverness fails the second as surely as a template fails the first. Earned-simple is the only legal exception, and it must be argued in the take's reason.
