# Worked Example — Ravila Grand Hotel

One brand, reasoned start to finish, **including the wrong turns**. The wrong turns are not edited out — they are the most useful part. A junior is taught the clean answer; an apprentice is shown the tempting mistakes and why a master didn't make them.

Read this next to `SKILL.md`. That file is the method; this is the method *happening*.

---

## The brand, as the research handed it over

Ravila Grand Hotel. The founder wants one thing: more **direct room bookings** from people searching online — strangers, not events, not banquets.

The website's own words: *"Premium Business Hotel in Madinaguda. Save 50% vs HITEC City Hotels. Preferred by Microsoft, Amazon, TCS professionals. Quiet rooms for video calls."* A clean, modern, SEO-built site aimed squarely at the IT corridor.

If I had stopped there, the strategy writes itself: a business hotel near the tech corridor, test three messages at business travellers — location, value, proof. Tidy. **And wrong.** Here is how the research stopped me.

## The wrong turn I didn't take — inheriting the self-portrait

The website is a *claim*. So I put it next to the rest of the file.

- The **reviews** said "clean, friendly, *adequate* budget hotel" — guests praised value and staff, never luxury. The site's "₹1,200 breakfast" claim was flatly contradicted by guests saying there was no restaurant.
- The **photos** showed budget-tier rooms — and a large share of them staged with **birthday balloons, heart-shaped towels, party lighting.** A business traveller taking video calls does not want a purple-LED party ceiling. Those rooms were dressed for *celebrations*.
- The site quietly **hid** that the property runs two banquet halls — a real, active events business.
- The "15 minutes to HITEC City" claim: the hotel's own commute page admitted 12–15 km and 25–60 minutes in traffic. That is **not** corridor-adjacent.

The self-portrait and the reality disagreed on almost every axis. Reality wins. **This is not a business hotel with a marketing problem. It is a budget hotel — whose real demand looks like celebrations, families, and events — wearing a "premium business hotel" costume on its website.**

Lesson: if I had trusted the homepage, I would have spent the founder's whole budget attracting corridor business travellers — the one customer this hotel, sitting 45 minutes away in traffic, structurally *loses*.

## Pinning the win — and finding the warm/cold gap

Conversion event: a direct room booking. Simple.

But reading the research *through* that lens exposed the real problem. Every room Ravila fills today comes from people who **already know it** — banquet guests, regulars, word of mouth. The founder now wants **cold strangers** from Meta ads. The hotel has *no proven ability to convert a cold stranger*. That is a different funnel entirely. Naming it was half the diagnosis.

## The diagnosis — and what kind of test this is

The blocker: **Ravila is invisible to every cold room-seeker it could win — and nobody, the founder included, knows which of those seekers is actually its market.**

Notice the shape of that. The unknown is not *what to say*. The unknown is *who*. So this is not a message test. It is an **audience-discovery test**: competing bets about which kind of room-seeker Ravila can win cold.

And the honest flag, named up front: even a perfect ad hits a wall — a stranger who clicks will Google Ravila and find near-zero reviews on the OTAs strangers actually check, plus "premium" claims guests contradict. Creative wins the click; that trust gap caps the booking. Not an angle — a precondition the founder must hear.

## Prescribing — generate, then screen

The property is in Madinaguda, near the Miyapur/Kukatpally IT-*residential* belt, with a hospital directly across the road. Generating candidate "who"s was cheap; I listed five:

1. Visiting family of IT workers who live in the belt
2. Families of patients at the hospital across the road
3. Couples/families wanting an affordable celebration night
4. Relocation / new-joiner landing-pad stays
5. Industrial-corridor business travel (the property sits on NH65 toward the Patancheru industrial belt)

**Gate 1 — can it run as a clean test?**

Segment 5, *industrial business travel*, failed. "Visiting a plant in Patancheru?" is a narrow B2B trigger, the room is often booked by the company not the traveller, and there is no scroll-stopping in-feed moment. It failed self-selection. It is a real demand source — but it cannot be *tested cleanly on Meta*. Killed. (Note this carefully: a segment can be genuine and still fail the gate. The gate is about testability, not merit.)

The other four passed — though medical's volume and relocation's lumpy seasonal volume both got flagged as marginal.

**Gate 2 — worth a slot?**

- *Celebration* — product fit is excellent: the rooms are *already staged for this*. Strong intent, dated. Lower learning (we can half-see it works). → the safe **anchor**.
- *Family-visit* — honest fit, good intent, *high learning* (genuinely unproven cold). → a core unknown.
- *Medical-stay* — honest fit, *strongest intent of all* (a hospital admission is non-discretionary, now), high learning. Soft spot: marginal volume.
- *Relocation* — weakest fit (a flat-hunter prefers a service apartment to a two-week hotel room), lumpy volume, and it **overlaps** family-visit (both are "I need a base in the Miyapur belt").

**Portfolio check.** Drop **Relocation** — weakest fit, overlaps another survivor. The final three — Celebration, Family-visit, Medical-stay — are three genuinely different humans, no overlap, and nicely hedged: one likely anchor plus two real unknowns.

The one sentence underneath them all: **Ravila wins reason-driven proximity bookings, not destination-choice bookings.** Nobody in this set is "shopping Hyderabad hotels" — each needs a room near a fixed point for a specific life reason. That is the lane, and it is the honest one.

## The wrong turn I nearly took — letting an event hijack the test

The calendar research (anchored to today's real date, filtered to the test window) came back: the window is front-loaded dead and back-loaded busy, with a cluster of B2B trade expos at HITEX in late June.

The tempting move: make a HITEX-expo cell a fourth angle — it would resurrect the industrial segment I'd killed (a named expo *is* the sharp trigger that segment lacked).

I didn't. Here's why: this is an *audience-discovery* test. The expo audience is not a discovery question — the calendar research already confirmed it exists. Dropping a tactical expo cell into a discovery test corrupts the read (if it wins, you've learned "expos drive demand," which you knew) and burns a slot. The expo is real money — but it is a *separate tactical burst*, not part of this experiment. The calendar's real job here is **timing**: run the discovery test through the busy late-June window, not the dead first half, or the test returns noise.

## Right-sizing and the mandatories

Assume the budget supports N=3. (If it only supported 2, the cut would be Medical — its volume is the most fragile — leaving Celebration + Family-visit, the two most readable.)

The mandatory is the same for all three: put the real room on screen — it is a hotel, the room *is* the product, and the cell builds the actual look, not me. I don't assign each a mood; they are already distinct by *who and why* — a celebration night, a family visit, and a hospital stay are three different rooms before they are three different ads.

## What this example is meant to teach

Every line of the resulting Bet is the output of a *move*, not a template. The value was never the three angles — it was the refusals: refusing the website's self-portrait, refusing to test variants before discovering the audience, refusing to let a shiny event corrupt the experiment, refusing to stay quiet about the review gap. A blender could not have produced this. That is the whole point.

# Worked Example — The Whole Truth Foods

A second brand, reasoned the same way — and chosen deliberately because almost nothing about its *shape* matches Ravila. A national D2C food brand, not a local service business. An established, well-funded company, not a cold-start. A self-portrait that turns out **true**, not a costume. And — as you'll see — a different *kind* of test falls out the other end. If the method only worked on hotels, this is where it would break. It didn't. Read it next to the Ravila case; the contrasts are the lesson.

## The brand, as the research handed it over

The Whole Truth Foods — an Indian D2C brand: protein bars, peanut butter, whey, muesli. Founded 2019. By 2026 it is large — ~₹216 Cr revenue, a $51M Series D, "public-market ready."

Its self-portrait is loud and consistent: *"We're re-building the world's trust in food. India's first 100% clean-label food brand."* Radical ingredient transparency — every ingredient, every percentage, on the front of the pack; a founder-led, anti-marketing voice.

With Ravila, the website was a costume hiding a different business. My first instinct here was to expect the same trick. **It wasn't there** — and noticing *that* was the move.

## The move — triangulation, when the self-portrait is true

I put the self-portrait next to the rest of the research, as always. And the reviews *agreed with it*: 4.3–4.6 across every platform, and the praise was specifically "clean, honest, transparent." The brand says it is honest; customers say it is honest. The self-portrait is **true**.

A junior stops there, relieved, and builds another transparency campaign. That is the trap — and Ravila did not teach it, because Ravila's lie was obvious once you looked. Here the danger is subtler: **a brand proud of a real strength keeps marketing that strength while its actual blocker sits unaddressed.**

So I kept triangulating — not for a lie, but for the *gap between what the brand talks about and what is actually stopping the sale*. It was right there:

- **Price is the #1 complaint** on every platform, every product line — and *intensifying*: a ~13% whey hike in early 2026 drew reviews explicitly threatening to churn.
- The live site carries a permanent banner — **"Our prices are changing. Why?"** — the brand is *publicly* managing a price-perception crisis.
- A second banner, "Discount auto-applied in cart" — quietly discounting, while positioned as a brand that doesn't.
- Category research is brutal on this: Indians pay ~20% more for clean, not 2–3×; the rational objection is "three eggs cost ₹30."

The brand talks about *trust*. The thing stopping the sale is *price*. That gap is the whole strategy.

## Pinning the win — and a fact I did not have

Conversion event: a purchase. But here I hit something Ravila never tested: **no founder in the loop.** No stated goal, no budget, no economics — Ravila's founder had told us "direct room bookings"; here, nothing.

The honest move is not to assume silently — it is to assume *out loud*. The price reviews scream a *retention* risk; a brand this funded is usually chasing *acquisition*. Different strategies. I could not resolve it from the research, so The Bet states the assumption plainly: **assuming the goal is new-customer acquisition**, with retention flagged as a parallel concern to confirm. A named assumption fails loud. An unnamed one fails silent.

## The diagnosis — and the honesty fork

The blocker: **a qualified, label-literate buyer believes TWT is honest — and balks at the price anyway.** The premium is not *justified* in their head, especially for repeat purchase.

The honesty fork: the price *number* and the post-hike repeat-cadence math are **not** creative-addressable — creative cannot un-raise a price. Price *perception* — the felt worth of the premium — **is**. So The Bet splits it honestly: the creative test reframes value; it does not fix the price ladder, and that half is flagged to the founder as a precondition, not an angle. (Same fork as Ravila's review-gap flag — different brand, same move.)

## What kind of test — and here the method turns

With Ravila the unknown was *who*, and round one became an audience-discovery test. Here, TWT knows its audience. The unknown is **what to say** to beat the price objection.

So this is a **message test**: one audience, competing value-justification messages — the *opposite shape* from Ravila. And it fell out of the same single move ("read what kind of uncertainty you face"). That is the proof the method is not hotel-shaped: it produced a different test design because the brand genuinely needed one.

## Prescribing — screening messages, not audiences

Because this is a *message* test, Gate 1's self-selection check mostly fell away (the audience is fixed) — Gate 1 was simply "on-strategy and expressible." Gate 2 and the portfolio check did the work. Three messages survived:

1. **Anti-adulteration insurance** — "cheap protein costs you in ways you can't see" (the 70%-mislabelled trust wound). Leverages TWT's real moat — the anchor.
2. **Honest indulgence** — defuse the clean-equals-joyless tradeoff; a treat worth paying for. A genuine unknown.
3. **Versus whole foods, head-on** — confront the eggs/dal objection nobody else dares. A genuine unknown.

Dropped: a pure cost-per-gram reframe (competes on price terms, risks cheapening the brand, overlaps #1) and a loyalty/repeat-economics message (a retention play — off-strategy for an acquisition test).

## Respecting an existing identity

Ravila had no real identity, so the cell would build close to from scratch. TWT is the opposite: a powerful, recognisable identity — playful hand-drawn doodles, bold display type, cream and maroon, anti-glossy, ingredient-forward. You do not invent a look that fights that.

So what I hand down is a mandatory, not a treatment: *stay inside TWT's existing identity*. I don't assign each angle a tone or art style — the three are already distinct by *who and what they promise*, and the cell dresses each inside the brand's system.

## What this example teaches

Set it beside Ravila and almost every surface is different: a lie vs a true self-portrait, an audience test vs a message test, a cold-start vs an established brand, from-scratch lanes vs treatments-within-an-identity, a missing founder vs a stated goal. **The surfaces changed completely. The moves did not** — same triangulation, same honesty fork, same "read the uncertainty," same generate-then-screen.

That is what a method *is*, and it is why the binder is one method with many examples — never one binder per vertical. This case is also the one that *hardened* the binder: running it surfaced five places the method had quietly over-fitted the Ravila shape, and those were corrected. A method earns trust by being run against something it was not written for — and surviving.

# Worked Example — TheRateFinder

The third brand — and the one that *changed the binder*. A Canadian mortgage lead-gen platform: a different country, a different conversion event (a *lead*, not a sale or a booking), an intangible service rather than a product. Two examples can still share a blind spot. A third corner is what finds one.

## The brand, as the research handed it over

TheRateFinder — theratefinder.ca. It calls itself "Canada's First Lending Hub," and its name and homepage promise consumer rate-shopping: *find your best mortgage rate*. Underneath, its own terms are explicit that it is a lead-gen marketplace — it captures a borrower's details and routes them to lenders.

## The move — triangulation, a third kind of gap

By now triangulation had a track record. Ravila's self-portrait was a *lie*. TWT's was *true but not the blocker*. TheRateFinder's was a third thing again: **the self-portrait is at war with itself.**

The name and homepage point at consumer rate-shopping — the single most commoditized, most crowded corner of the category (every aggregator, every bank, ~310 competing renewal ads on Meta). But the product depth, and the brand's *own active ads*, point the opposite way — commercial, construction, private and developer lending, an uncontested lane no competitor advertises in. The brand's name fights the brand's strategy. Same move, third kind of finding.

## Pin the win — and a brand with no warm anything

No founder in the loop — so, assumption stated out loud, the goal is lead acquisition and the conversion event is a mortgage lead.

But pinning the win surfaced the real situation: TheRateFinder has *zero* warm base — no reviews on any platform, a dormant Facebook page, ad campaigns only weeks old. It is not merely cold-acquisition-dependent like Ravila; it is **pre-reputation entirely.** Every lead must come from a stranger who has never heard of it.

## Diagnose — and the honesty fork carries the whole case

A qualified Canadian with a mortgage need meets TheRateFinder and does not hand over their financial details. Why? Because it is a brand-new, faceless funnel in a trust-critical, regulated category — showing **no named brokers, no licence disclosure, zero third-party reviews, and rates dated "May 2026" that are really August 2025 numbers.** (Date-anchoring caught the stale rates: a strategist anchored to the real date sees a nine-month-old figure dressed as current.) The category research is blunt — the number-one reason a Canadian abandons a mortgage lead form is "my data gets sold / the rate isn't real." Competitors advertise "16,000 five-star reviews." TheRateFinder has none.

Then the honesty fork — and here it produced not a footnote but the *whole* diagnosis. **Almost none of this is creative-addressable.** No ad manufactures a licence, a named human, a review base, or a current rate. The blocker is legitimacy, and legitimacy is not a creative problem.

## The move the binder did not yet have — judging readiness

For Ravila and TWT, the non-creative issue was a real flaw but a *flag* — a test still taught you something. Here it was not a flag; it was the entire story. A flawless ad pointed at this funnel still loses, because the stranger who clicks bounces off a destination they have no reason to trust.

That is a second kind of un-runnable test — not "the budget is too small to read a result" but "**the destination leaks; no creative converts into it.**" The binder, at the time of this run, only knew the budget kind — so the honest output had no clean home in the method. That is exactly what a third example is *for*. The binder gained a move out of this run: **judge whether the brand is ready to be marketed**, and when it is not, flip The Bet's shape.

## What this example teaches

Three brands, three shapes — a hotel, a packaged-goods brand, a financial lead-gen platform; a lie, an over-marketed truth, a brand at war with itself; an audience test, a message test, and a test that should not run yet. **The surfaces could not differ more. The moves did not change** — triangulation, pin-the-win, the honesty fork, date-anchoring all held.

But this run did what the first two could not: it found a hole. The binder had no move for a brand that is not ready to be marketed, and no answer for the founder who says "run it anyway." A method written from one example inherits that example's shape; a method run against a third, genuinely different example finds the shape it was missing — and earns a new move. That is the worked example doing its real job: not decorating the method, but **stress-testing it until it breaks, then mending it.**
