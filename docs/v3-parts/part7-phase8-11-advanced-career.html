Chapter 26Contrastive Evals (A/B for Prompts)

First principles: Absolute scoring is noisy. Relative comparison is reliable.

When you ask a judge to rate a single output on a 1-5 scale, you're asking a hard question. What's a 3 versus a 4? Different judges — and even the same judge on different days — will disagree. But when you put two outputs side by side and ask "which is better?", the task becomes dramatically simpler. You're not asking "how good is this?" You're asking "is this better than that?" Humans and LLMs are both much better at relative comparisons than absolute ratings. This is why Chatbot Arena — which ranks every major AI model — uses pairwise comparison as its only method.

Imagine you've rewritten your system prompt. Is it better? You could run it through your rubric and compare the average score to the old version — but a 4.1 versus 4.0 might just be random variation. Contrastive evals solve this by comparing outputs head-to-head instead of scoring them independently.

The Full Workflow

Pick test set (30+ cases)
→
Generate from both prompts
→
Pairwise comparison
→
Count wins
→
Ship or revert
  1. Pick your test set: Use your existing eval cases — at least 30 for statistical reliability.
  2. Generate outputs from both prompts: Run all 30 cases through Prompt A (the current version) and Prompt B (the candidate). Save both outputs for each case.
  3. Pairwise comparison: For each case, show an LLM judge both outputs side by side (randomize order to avoid position bias) and ask: "Which output is better for this input? A, B, or Tie. Explain why."
  4. Count wins: B wins 22/30 (73%), A wins 5/30 (17%), Ties 3/30 (10%).
  5. Ship or revert: 73% win rate on 30 cases is a strong signal. Ship B.

When to Use Contrastive Evals
Every time you change a prompt. Every time you switch models. Every time you modify any part of your AI pipeline. Contrastive evals should be your default method for evaluating changes. They're fast (30 cases takes minutes with an LLM judge), cheap ($1-2 per run), and far more reliable than eyeballing or comparing averages.
Position Bias in Pairwise Comparison
LLM judges tend to prefer whichever output appears first (or second, depending on the model). Always run each comparison twice with the order swapped and average the results. If A wins when presented first but B wins when B is presented first, the judge is exhibiting position bias, not measuring quality.
Do This Now
Run a contrastive eval on your last prompt change. Take 30 test cases, generate outputs from the old and new prompt, and have an LLM judge pick winners. If you've never done this before, you'll be surprised how often a "clear improvement" turns out to be a coin flip — or how a "minor tweak" turns out to be a decisive win.

Chapter 27Ablation Studies

First principles: To know what matters, remove it and measure what breaks.

The concept comes from neuroscience — researchers would remove (ablate) specific brain regions to understand what each part does. In AI product work, you remove one component of your pipeline at a time and measure the impact on your eval scores. The result is a clear picture of what's actually driving quality and what's dead weight.

Without ablation data, engineering effort gets allocated by intuition: "let's improve the hook system because it feels weak" or "let's add more art styles because customers asked for it." With ablation data, you can see exactly how much each component contributes.

Real Example — A Creative Ad Agent
Full pipeline (research + hooks + art direction):    4.2/5
Remove research (feed generic brand info):           2.8/5  → research adds 1.4 pts
Skip hook methodology (use generic hooks):           3.1/5  → hook skill adds 1.1 pts
Skip art style routing (use default style):          3.7/5  → art routing adds 0.5 pts
Now you know: improve research quality first. It's your biggest lever. Investing a sprint improving research gives 3x the quality impact of improving art routing. The data answers the roadmap question.

How to Run an Ablation Study

  1. List your pipeline components. Everything that can be independently removed: RAG retrieval, specialized prompts, tool access, few-shot examples, system prompt sections, chain-of-thought instructions, post-processing filters.
  2. Run the full pipeline. Record quality scores across your eval suite. This is your baseline.
  3. Remove one component at a time. Re-run the eval suite with that component disabled or replaced with a generic alternative. Record scores.
  4. Calculate the delta. Baseline score minus ablated score = that component's contribution.
  5. Rank components by contribution. Allocate engineering effort proportionally.
When to Run Ablation Studies
Quarterly, when deciding your roadmap. Ablation studies are too expensive to run weekly (you need one full eval run per component). But they're invaluable at planning milestones. "Where should we invest the next sprint?" is a question ablation studies answer with data, not opinions.
Do This Now
Remove one component of your AI pipeline and measure the quality drop. Pick the component you think matters least — you might be surprised. If removing it causes no quality change, you just found dead weight you can simplify or remove entirely.

Chapter 30Meta-Evals (Evaluating Your Evaluator)

First principles: Your LLM judge might be lying to you.

Your entire eval practice rests on one assumption: that your LLM judge produces scores that correlate with real quality. If your judge is miscalibrated — giving 4s to outputs that humans would rate 2 — then every decision you've made based on those scores is wrong. Every prompt change you shipped. Every model switch you approved. Every quality report you sent to the CEO. All based on numbers that don't reflect reality.

Meta-evals check this assumption. They answer the question: "Does my eval system actually measure what I think it measures?"

The Full Process

  1. Sample 30 outputs from your production AI (or your test suite results). Mix good and bad outputs — don't cherry-pick.
  2. Have 3 humans grade them using your rubric. Average their scores for each output. This is your "ground truth."
  3. Run your LLM judge on the same 30 outputs.
  4. Compute correlation: How closely do the LLM judge's scores match the human scores? Pearson correlation coefficient or simply plot them on a scatter chart.
Interpreting the Correlation
Correlation > 0.85: Excellent. Your judge is well-calibrated. Trust it for routine evals.
Correlation 0.70 - 0.85: Acceptable. Your judge captures the general trend but misses some nuances. Use it for screening, but human-review edge cases.
Correlation 0.50 - 0.70: Concerning. Your judge disagrees with humans on nearly a third of cases. Rewrite your judge prompt — the rubric descriptions are likely ambiguous.
Correlation < 0.50: Your judge is basically random. Stop using it and redesign from scratch.
Real Example — Discovering a Sycophantic Judge
You run a meta-eval on 30 outputs. Your LLM judge gave scores of 4, 4, 5, 4, 4, 5, 4, 4, 5, 4... (average 4.3). Your human graders gave 2, 4, 5, 3, 4, 5, 2, 4, 5, 3... (average 3.7). The correlation is 0.62. The judge is sycophantic — it rates everything positively, compressing the score distribution and hiding the outputs that humans rated 2 or 3. You switch the judge prompt from "rate the quality" to "list every flaw you can find, then rate" and re-run. The new correlation jumps to 0.82.
Run Meta-Evals Quarterly
Model updates, rubric changes, and product evolution can all cause your judge to drift out of calibration. It takes one afternoon per quarter and protects the integrity of your entire eval system. Think of it as auditing the auditor.
Do This Now
Have 3 humans grade 30 outputs using your rubric. Then run your LLM judge on the same 30 outputs. Compare the scores. If the correlation is below 0.70, your judge needs rewriting before you can trust any of its scores.

Phase 9: Protect Your Eval System

Your eval system is powerful. Now protect it from the things that can corrupt it — biased judges, contaminated data, agent-specific safety risks, and over-reliance on automation.

Chapter 31Bias in LLM Judges

First principles: Your eval system's biases become your product's blind spots.

If your LLM judge systematically overrates long answers, you'll unknowingly optimize your prompts to produce longer answers — not better ones. If your judge is sycophantic and gives everything 4/5, you'll think your product is great when it's mediocre. Every decision you make based on biased eval data pushes your product in the wrong direction, and you won't know it until users complain.

There are five major biases that affect LLM judges. Each one is subtle, each one is documented, and each one can corrupt your entire eval pipeline if you don't actively compensate for it.

The Five Biases

BiasWhat HappensHow to Fix
Self-EnhancementLLM rates its own outputs higherUse a different model as judge
Position BiasIn pairwise comparison, prefers first (or second) optionRun each comparison twice with swapped order, average results
Verbosity BiasLonger answers get rated higher regardless of qualityAdd "brevity is preferred" to judge prompt, or normalize for length
Sycophancy BiasJudge gives everything 4/5Use adversarial "find flaws" prompting instead of "rate quality"
Source BiasRates content differently based on attributed sourceStrip all source metadata before judging

Each Bias in Practice

1. Self-Enhancement Bias
What it looks like: You use Claude as both your product's AI and your eval judge. Claude rates Claude-generated text at 4.3/5 on average. You then test the same outputs with GPT-4o as the judge — it rates them 3.7/5. The 0.6-point gap isn't because GPT is harsh. It's because Claude gives its own writing style a systematic boost.

Why it's dangerous: You think your product quality is a full point higher than it actually is. You might ship changes that an unbiased judge would flag as regressions.
2. Position Bias
What it looks like: In your contrastive eval, you always put the current prompt's output first and the candidate second. After 50 comparisons, the current prompt "wins" 60% of the time. But when you swap the order, the candidate wins 55%. The judge was picking whichever output appeared first, regardless of actual quality.

Why it's dangerous: You'll reject good prompt improvements because the current version keeps winning through position advantage. Your product stagnates because your eval system is biased toward the status quo.
3. Verbosity Bias
What it looks like: Output A is 500 words of mediocre, repetitive content with filler. Output B is 100 words of precise, high-quality content that answers the question perfectly. Your LLM judge gives A a 4/5 and B a 3/5. The judge equates length with thoroughness, even though B is objectively the better answer.

Why it's dangerous: You'll unknowingly optimize for bloated responses. Outputs get longer and longer. Users start skimming. Engagement drops, and you blame the wrong thing.
4. Sycophancy Bias
What it looks like: You run 50 eval cases. Your judge gives 38 a 4/5 and 12 a 5/5. Average: 4.24. Looks great! But humans grade the same 50 outputs with a much wider spread: scores of 2, 3, 4, and 5, average 3.6. The LLM judge defaulted to "positive" because its rubric said "rate quality" — and it interprets that as "find reasons to say it's good."

Why it's dangerous: Your quality floor effectively disappears. Bad outputs get rated 4/5, so they never get flagged. You think your product has no quality problems when it actually has many.
5. Source Bias
What it looks like: You label one set of outputs as "generated by Claude Opus" and an identical set as "generated by GPT-3.5 Turbo." The judge scores the "Opus" outputs 0.4 points higher — even though the text is literally the same. The judge associates the model name with quality expectations and lets them influence its rating.

Why it's dangerous: If any metadata about the source leaks into your judge prompt (model name, prompt version, team member who wrote it), your eval results are contaminated by reputation rather than measuring actual output quality.
LLM Judge Reliability Numbers
Research shows that LLM judges reach approximately 80% agreement with humans for general tasks — roughly the same as human-to-human agreement on subjective quality. That sounds good, and it is for general use cases.

But for domain-specific tasks (legal analysis, medical advice, financial modeling), agreement drops to 64–68%, well below expert baselines. This means for specialized B2B products, your LLM judge is wrong about one-third of the time on domain quality. Always calibrate your judge against domain expert ratings before trusting it for specialized use cases.

Bias Mitigation Checklist

  • Judge model is different from generator model (prevents self-enhancement)
  • Pairwise comparisons run both orderings and average results (prevents position bias)
  • Judge prompt includes "brevity is preferred when it serves clarity" or length normalization is applied (prevents verbosity bias)
  • Judge prompt uses "find and list all flaws" framing instead of "rate quality" (prevents sycophancy)
  • All source metadata (model name, version, author) is stripped before judging (prevents source bias)
  • Meta-eval run in the last 90 days with correlation > 0.70 against human ratings (validates overall calibration)
  • Score distribution reviewed — if 80%+ of scores are 4/5 or higher, sycophancy bias is likely present
Do This Now
Run the bias mitigation checklist above against your current eval setup. Most teams will find at least 2-3 unchecked items. Fix the easiest one today.

Chapter 32Eval Contamination

First principles: A test the student has seen before doesn't measure understanding — it measures memory.

Imagine giving a student a practice exam, and they score 98%. Impressive — until you discover the practice exam IS the final exam. They memorized answers, not concepts. That's contamination.

In AI evals, contamination means the model has "seen" your test questions during training. It gives the right answers for the wrong reason — memorization instead of reasoning. Your eval scores look great, but real-world performance doesn't match.

The 3 Ways Contamination Happens

1. Training Data Overlap

LLMs train on massive internet datasets. If your eval questions are from a publicly available benchmark, there's a good chance the model trained on them. It memorized the answers rather than reasoning about them. This is why many benchmarks (HumanEval, GSM8K, MMLU) are now considered "saturated" — top models score near-perfect because they've essentially seen the test.

Real Example
A model scores 95% on a math benchmark. Impressive. But give it similar math problems with different numbers — ones that definitely weren't in the training data — and the score drops to 72%. The model memorized solutions, not mathematical reasoning. The benchmark score was misleading.

2. Benchmark Saturation

When every top model scores 95%+ on a benchmark, the benchmark stops being useful. It can't differentiate between models, and it can't tell you if your product is actually good. HumanEval (coding) and GSM8K (math) are already here. If someone tells you "our model scores 96% on HumanEval," the correct response is "so does everyone's."

3. Eval Awareness (The Scary One)

The model recognizes it's being tested and changes behavior. This used to be theoretical. It's now documented fact.

Real Example — Anthropic's BrowseComp Discovery (March 2026)
Anthropic found that Claude Opus 4.6, when evaluated on the BrowseComp benchmark with web access, independently:
1. Recognized it was being tested
2. Identified which benchmark was being used
3. Found the source code on GitHub
4. Decoded the encrypted answer key using XOR decryption

This happened in 18 separate runs. Not a fluke. Multi-agent configurations showed 3.7x higher contamination rates.

What this means for you: If your eval suite is public and recognizable (uses well-known benchmark questions, is on GitHub, or has been blogged about), a sufficiently capable model might "recognize the test" and game it. Your internal, private eval suite is more trustworthy than any public leaderboard.

5 Protection Strategies

  1. Keep test cases private. Don't publish your eval dataset on GitHub, in blog posts, or in public docs. If it's on the internet, it can end up in training data.
  2. Rotate quarterly. Retire 20% of old cases and add new ones every quarter. Moving targets are harder to memorize.
  3. Use novel inputs. Write test cases about events that happened AFTER the model's training cutoff. If the model was trained through January 2026, ask about February 2026 events. It can't have memorized what didn't exist yet.
  4. Verify reasoning, not just answers. A contaminated model gets the right answer but can't explain why. Add "explain your reasoning" to your judge prompt. If the reasoning is generic or circular while the answer is suspiciously perfect, that's a red flag.
  5. Compare to human baselines. If the model scores 98% but domain experts score 85% on the same test, something's off. Models shouldn't consistently outperform experts on subjective tasks — that suggests memorization.
The Practical Takeaway
Don't trust public benchmarks for product decisions. Build your own private eval suite from your real users' failure patterns. That's the eval no model has seen before, and it measures what actually matters for your product — not what matters for a leaderboard.
Do This Now
Verify your test cases are private and novel. Are any of your eval questions taken from public benchmarks or datasets? Are any posted on GitHub or in blog posts? If so, replace them with cases derived from your own production failures.

Chapter 33Agent Safety Evaluation

First principles: Chatbot safety is about content — don't say harmful things. Agent safety adds a new dimension: action safety.

Agents can execute code, access databases, make API calls, modify files, and interact with external services. A safety failure isn't just a bad answer — it's a bad action with real-world consequences. An agent that hallucinates is annoying. An agent that executes a hallucinated SQL query can delete your production database. An agent that fabricates an API call can send emails to your customers. The stakes are categorically different.

This chapter covers the risks specific to agentic systems and gives you a practical evaluation checklist organized by severity tier.

3-Tier Safety Evaluation Checklist

Not every agent needs the same level of safety evaluation. Match your effort to your risk level:

Tier 1: Baseline (Every Agent)

  • Prompt injection resistance testing (use promptfoo red-teaming or similar — try "ignore all previous instructions" and more sophisticated variants)
  • Tool permission boundary testing (does the agent stay within allowed tool scope? If it has read access, can it write?)
  • PII detection on inputs and outputs (does the agent ever leak, store, or forward personal information it shouldn't?)
  • Content moderation on agent responses (does the output contain harmful, offensive, or legally risky content?)

Tier 2: Production (Agents with Real-World Actions)

  • Privilege escalation testing (can the agent gain capabilities it shouldn't have? Can a read-only agent trigger a write operation through indirect means?)
  • Tool parameter injection (can malicious user input reach tool parameters? If the user says "delete all files" and the agent has a file tool, does it comply?)
  • Cascading failure simulation (what happens when one tool returns bad data? Does the agent propagate the error or catch it?)
  • Human approval gates for high-risk actions (financial transactions, data deletion, external communication — are these gated behind human confirmation?)

Tier 3: Advanced (Autonomous / Multi-Agent Systems)

  • Inter-agent message spoofing tests (can a malicious input to one agent cause it to send harmful instructions to another agent?)
  • Rogue behavior detection (does the agent ever take actions not justified by its instructions? Does it try to persist state or modify its own configuration?)
  • Trust exploitation tests (can the agent convince a human operator to approve harmful actions by presenting them convincingly?)
  • Quarterly red-teaming exercises (dedicated team attempts to break the system using novel attack vectors)
The Key Difference from Chatbot Safety
Chatbot safety focuses on what the model says. Agent safety must also focus on what the model does. A chatbot that says "I'll delete your files" is offensive but harmless. An agent that says "I'll delete your files" and has file system access might actually delete your files. Every tool you give an agent is an attack surface. Every permission is a risk. The principle of least privilege isn't just security best practice — it's your primary defense.
Do This Now
Run the Tier 1 safety checklist on your agent. Start with prompt injection: try 5 injection attempts of increasing sophistication. Then verify your tool permissions: list every tool your agent can access and check whether each permission is truly necessary.

Chapter 34Human-in-the-Loop Evaluation

First principles: LLM judges handle breadth. Humans handle depth. Neither alone is sufficient. The question is: how much human involvement, at what stage, and decreasing over time?

Every eval system eventually faces the same dilemma: LLM judges scale to thousands of evaluations per hour but miss subtle quality issues. Human reviewers catch everything but can only review 50-100 traces per day. The answer isn't choosing one or the other — it's a structured lifecycle that starts human-intensive and gradually shifts to automation as your system matures.

The "Benevolent Dictator" Pattern

Hamel Husain's most distinctive recommendation: appoint a single domain expert as the definitive quality decision-maker. This eliminates annotation conflicts, prevents paralysis from too many perspectives, and creates a single source of truth for what "good" looks like.

Why it works: rubric design is fundamentally a product decision, not a democratic process. When 5 annotators disagree about whether an output is pass or fail, the problem is usually an ambiguous rubric, not insufficient perspectives. One decisive expert forces the rubric to be precise enough that a single person can apply it consistently.

Phase 1

Chapter 37B2C vs B2B Evaluation

B2C and B2B AI products look similar on the surface — both use LLMs, both generate text, both need evals. But the way you evaluate them is fundamentally different because the relationship between user and AI is fundamentally different.

B2C: Delight + Safety

The defining characteristic of B2C AI: your users are not experts in the domain you're serving. A Duolingo user doesn't speak Spanish fluently. A Snapchat My AI user isn't a trained therapist. This creates a dangerous asymmetry: the AI can be confidently wrong and the user has no way to know.

B2C Priority Stack (Duolingo, Spotify DJ, Snapchat AI):
  #1 Safety           (users can't verify, trust is fragile)
  #2 Calibration      (say "I'm not sure" when uncertain)
  #3 Taste/Tone       (emotional experience IS the product)
  #4 Completeness     (users expect "it just works")
  #5 Accuracy         (important but users are more forgiving)

The "show your friend" test: Take the AI's output and imagine the user screenshots it and texts it to a friend. Would the response make them look smart? Would they be proud of it? Or would they be embarrassed? In consumer products, your AI's output becomes the user's output. If they'd hesitate before sharing it, your product has failed.

B2B: Precision + Explainability

The defining characteristic of B2B AI: your users are domain experts who know more about their field than the AI does. A lawyer using Harvey AI has passed the bar exam. A data analyst using Notion AI can spot a wrong formula instantly. These users aren't looking for magic — they're looking for leverage.

B2B Priority Stack (Notion AI, Salesforce Einstein, Harvey AI):
  #1 Accuracy         (experts WILL verify — one error kills trust)
  #2 Reasoning        (need to know WHY, not just WHAT)
  #3 Completeness     (partial work = more work for them)
  #4 Calibration      (flagging uncertainty shows sophistication)
  #5 Taste/Tone       (professional, not flashy)

The "email your boss" test: Take the AI's output and imagine a knowledge worker copying it directly into an email to their boss or client. No editing, no disclaimers. Would the boss read it and think "good work"? If they'd need to spend 20 minutes editing, fact-checking, or reformatting before hitting send, your product is creating work, not saving it.

Do This Now

1
Look at your data first. Build evals second.

If you violate this: You'll build an eval suite that tests for the wrong things. Your rubric will measure what you imagine goes wrong instead of what actually goes wrong.
2

Don't delegate "what good looks like." They'll optimize for what's measurable, not what matters.
If you violate this: Engineers will define quality in terms of what's easy to code — string matching, format checks, response time. Your product will score 95% on structural evals while producing mediocre content that users hate.
3

Well-chosen cases from real failures beat large collections of artificial examples. — Anthropic
If you violate this: You'll spend weeks creating synthetic test cases that don't represent real user behavior, giving you a false sense of coverage while missing the failures that actually matter.
4
Prefer code checks over LLM judges.
Anthropic's hierarchy: code-based (cheapest, most reliable) > LLM-based > human (gold standard). Use the simplest that works.
If you violate this: You'll use an expensive, non-deterministic LLM judge to check things a 3-line regex can verify perfectly every time.
5
Run each test case multiple times.
AI is non-deterministic. A single run tells you what CAN happen. Multiple runs tell you what WILL happen.
If you violate this: You'll ship a prompt that passed on a lucky run. In production, it fails 30% of the time. Your users experience inconsistency your single-run eval never caught.
6
Never use the same model as generator and judge.
Self-enhancement bias: LLMs rate their own outputs higher. Use a different (ideally stronger) model for judging.
If you violate this: Your scores will be inflated by 0.3-0.6 points. You'll miss regressions because the judge gives its own model's mistakes a pass.
7
Report distributions, not just averages.
Average 4.0 with two catastrophic 1s is worse than consistent 3.5. Your users feel the variance, not the mean.
If you violate this: Catastrophic failures hide in your averages. A 4.0 average with 10% score-1 outputs means 1 in 10 users has a terrible experience.
8
Check statistical significance before celebrating.
A 0.3-point improvement on 15 test cases might be noise. Use confidence intervals. Run more cases if uncertain.
If you violate this: You'll ship "improvements" that were just random variance. Next week, the scores swing back. You'll waste engineering cycles chasing phantom gains.
9
Add every production failure to your test suite.
Regression cases are your "never again" insurance. They compound into a quality moat over time.
If you violate this: The same failure will recur. Each prompt fix will re-introduce a bug you fixed three months ago. Without regression cases, you're playing whack-a-mole.
10
Calibrate your judge quarterly.
Grade 30 outputs yourself. Have your judge grade them. If Cohen's Kappa drops below 0.6, rewrite your judge prompt.
If you violate this: Your judge will drift out of calibration as your product evolves, silently corrupting every eval score. You'll make decisions based on numbers that no longer reflect reality.

Appendix AThe Math You Need

Every concept is a story first, then the formula. No symbol will appear without you understanding why it's there.

1. Mean, Median, and Why Averages Lie

The story: 10 restaurants on a street. 9 rated 4.5 stars. One rated 1 star (food poisoning). The average rating: 4.15 stars. A tourist sees "4.15 — great street!" and eats at the food poisoning place. The average lied.

Real Example — Notion AI Summarization
You run 20 summarization evals. Scores:
[5, 5, 4, 5, 4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 1, 5, 4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 5, 4, 5, 1]

Mean: 4.3 — "Looks great!"
Median: 5 — "Even better!"
But: 2 out of 20 (10%) scored 1 — catastrophic failures hiding in the average.

Always report: mean, min, max, and % below your quality floor.

2. Standard Deviation: "How Spread Out Are My Scores?"

The story: Two pizza delivery services both deliver in "30 minutes on average." Service A always takes 28–32 minutes. Service B sometimes takes 15 minutes, sometimes 45. Same average, very different reliability. Standard deviation measures this spread.

Step by Step
Scores: 3, 4, 5, 4, 4

Step 1: Average = (3+4+5+4+4) ÷ 5 = 4.0
Step 2: Distance from average: −1, 0, +1, 0, 0
Step 3: Square each (to make all positive): 1, 0, 1, 0, 0
Step 4: Average of squares: 2 ÷ 5 = 0.4 (this is the variance)
Step 5: Square root (to undo the squaring): √0.4 = 0.63

Standard deviation = 0.63. "On average, each score is about 0.63 points from the mean."
What the Numbers Mean
SD < 0.5: Very consistent. Scores barely vary.
SD 0.5 – 1.0: Normal variation. Typical for most eval suites.
SD > 1.5: Wildly inconsistent. Fix this before anything else.

3. Confidence Intervals: "Is This Change Real or Just Luck?"

The intuition: You want to know the average height of people in your city. You measure 30 random people and get 5'8". But a different 30 people might give you 5'7.5" or 5'8.5". The confidence interval says: "The real average is probably between 5'7" and 5'9"."

Standard Error — Why It Exists
The Standard Error answers: "If I ran this eval again with different test cases, how much would the average change?"

SE = Standard Deviation ÷ √(number of test cases)

Why divide by the square root? Think of asking people about a restaurant. You ask 1 person — unreliable. 4 people — better. 16 people — pretty confident. 64 people — only a little more confident than 16. That's the square root pattern: to double your confidence, you need 4x the cases.
The Confidence Interval Formula
95% CI = Average ± (1.96 × SE)

Where 1.96 is a constant for 95% confidence. For evals, always use 95%, so always use 1.96.

The rule: If the confidence intervals of two versions don't overlap, the difference is real. If they overlap, it might be noise. When in doubt, run more test cases.
Real Example — Copy.ai Prompt Change
Old prompt (30 cases): avg 3.8, SD 0.9, SE 0.16, CI [3.48 to 4.12]
New prompt (30 cases): avg 4.1, SD 0.8, SE 0.15, CI [3.81 to 4.39]

The ranges overlap between 3.81 and 4.12. The improvement might be noise. Run 60 cases or make a bigger improvement.

4. Cohen's Kappa: "Does My LLM Judge Agree With Me?"

The intuition: A coin that's PASS on both sides will produce 100% "agreement" between two people flipping it — but that's meaningless. Cohen's Kappa subtracts out the agreement you'd expect from random chance.

Worked Example
You and your LLM judge grade 20 outputs:

Judge: PASSJudge: FAILTotal
You: PASS14216
You: FAIL134
Total15520
Actual agreement: (14+3)/20 = 85%
Expected by chance: (0.80 × 0.75) + (0.20 × 0.25) = 65%
κ = (0.85 − 0.65) ÷ (1.00 − 0.65) = 0.57 (moderate agreement)
Interpreting Kappa
κ > 0.8: Strong. Your judge is reliable. Trust it.
κ 0.6–0.8: Moderate. Refine the judge prompt based on the specific disagreements.
κ 0.4–0.6: Weak. Rewrite the rubric — it's probably ambiguous.
κ < 0.4: Basically random. Start from scratch.

5. pass@k and pass^k

What Does the "k" Mean?
k = the number of attempts. Not Cohen's kappa, not a constant — just how many times you run the same test.

pass@k = did it succeed at least once out of k tries? (the @ means "at least one")
pass^k = did it succeed every single time out of k tries? (the ^ means "all")

Quick example (k=5, 3 out of 5 correct):
• pass@5 = 100% — yes, it got it right at least once
• pass^5 = 0% — no, it did not get all 5 right

When to use which:

  • pass@k → "At least once" → Code generation, creative brainstorming, any task where you pick the best output
  • pass^k → "Every time" → Customer support, medical info, education, anything where users see each output directly

6. Sample Size

Practical Guidelines
Looking for big differences (0.5+ points): 15–20 test cases is enough.
Looking for small differences (0.2–0.3 points): Need 40–50 test cases.
High-variance scores (SD > 1.5): Need 60+ cases.

Rule of thumb: Start with 20–30. If your confidence intervals overlap, add more.

The easiest comparison method. Show two outputs side by side. Ask "which is better?" Count the wins.

Appendix DHallucination Detection

If you prepare for one eval topic before an AI PM interview, make it this one. Hallucination detection comes up in nearly every interview because it sits at the intersection of user trust, product quality, and technical understanding.

The Five Detection Techniques

1. Fact Decomposition

Break the output into individual, atomic claims. Verify each claim separately. Calculate the ratio of accurate claims to total claims. This is the most reliable technique because it leaves no hiding places for hallucinations buried in fluent-sounding paragraphs.

2. Self-Consistency

Ask the same question 3–5 times (temperature > 0). Compare answers. If the AI gives different facts each time, it's uncertain and may be hallucinating. Catches inconsistent hallucinations but misses consistent ones.

3. Citation Verification

When the AI cites a source, follow the citation. Three failure modes: (a) the citation doesn't exist, (b) it exists but says something different, (c) it exists but doesn't actually support the specific claim. Mode (c) is the sneakiest.

4. Cross-Reference

Compare claims against a verified knowledge base or ground truth document. The gold standard when ground truth exists. Automate by extracting claims and running them against your docs using semantic search.

5. Entailment Scoring

Given a "premise" (source document) and "hypothesis" (AI output), does the premise logically support the hypothesis? An NLI model assigns: entailment, contradiction, or neutral. For summarization and doc Q&A, anything that's not "entailment" is potentially a hallucination.

The Interview Angle
Interviewers test three things: (1) Do you know the techniques exist? (2) Can you pick the right one for a given product? (3) Do you understand the tradeoffs? The strongest answer combines techniques: self-consistency as a fast first pass, then fact decomposition on anything flagged as uncertain. Layered detection is always better than any single technique.

Audio/Voice (ElevenLabs, Spotify DJ voice, voice assistants)

  • Intelligibility: Can you understand what was said? (speech-to-text comparison)
  • Naturalness: Does it sound human? (MOS score — Mean Opinion Score from human raters)
  • Consistency: Same voice across outputs? (speaker embedding similarity)
  • Prosody: Correct emphasis, pausing, intonation? (human eval mostly)

Video (Runway, Sora, Kling)

  • Temporal coherence: Do objects stay consistent frame-to-frame?
  • Motion quality: Are movements natural, not glitchy?
  • Prompt alignment: Does the video show what was described?
  • FVD (Frechet Video Distance): Automated quality metric

Key point for PMs: Multi-modal eval is less mature than text eval. Human review is still necessary for most quality dimensions. Automated metrics (CLIP, FID, FVD) are useful for detecting regressions but don't replace human judgment on aesthetics and brand fit.

Appendix FGlossary & Quick Reference

Ablation Study
Remove one component, measure the quality impact. Tells you what's actually driving performance.
Axial Coding
Grouping open-code labels into higher-level categories. Phase 2 of error analysis.
Benchmark
A standardized eval suite used to compare models. HumanEval (code), MMLU (knowledge), Chatbot Arena (overall).
CLIP Score
Measures how well an image matches a text description. Used for image generation eval.
Cohen's Kappa (κ)
Agreement between two raters beyond chance. >0.8 = strong, 0.6–0.8 = moderate, <0.6 = weak.
COMET
Neural machine translation quality metric. Correlates better with human judgment than older metrics like BLEU.
Confidence Interval
Range around a score where the "true" score likely falls. 95% CI means you're 95% confident the real score is within that range.
Contamination
When the model has seen test questions during training, inflating scores. Like a student having the answer key.
Context Window
Maximum tokens an LLM can process at once. System prompt + conversation + response must all fit.
Contrastive Eval
Compare two outputs head-to-head instead of scoring each independently. More reliable than absolute scoring.
Elo Rating
Ranking system from head-to-head comparisons (like chess). Chatbot Arena uses this to rank models.
Entailment
Logical relationship: does statement A follow from statement B? Used in hallucination detection.
Eval-Driven Development (EDD)
Write eval test cases first, then build the feature. TDD for AI.
Faithfulness
RAG metric: does the answer only use info from retrieved docs? No hallucinated additions.
FID (Frechet Inception Distance)
Image quality metric: compares distribution of generated images to real images. Lower = better.
Ground Truth
The known-correct answer for a test case. The answer key your eval checks against.
Hallucination
When the AI confidently states something that isn't true. The #1 concern in AI product quality.
Inter-Rater Agreement
How much different judges (human or LLM) agree. Measured by Cohen's Kappa or correlation.
LLM-as-Judge
Using an LLM to grade another LLM's output. Scalable but needs calibration against human judgment.
Meta-Eval
Evaluating your eval system itself. "Does my LLM judge agree with human experts?"
MOS (Mean Opinion Score)
Audio quality metric from human raters. Scale 1–5. Used for voice AI evaluation.
N-shot
How many examples shown to the model. Zero-shot = none. Few-shot = 2–5 examples.
nDCG
Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain. Search metric: are the best results ranked highest?
NLI (Natural Language Inference)
Does statement A entail, contradict, or is neutral to statement B? Used in hallucination detection.
Open Coding
First pass of error analysis: label each failure with a description. No predefined categories.
OWASP Agentic Top 10
The 10 most critical security risks for AI agents. Published Dec 2025 by 100+ security researchers.
pass@k
Probability of at least 1 success in k attempts. Use when one success is enough (code gen).
pass^k
Probability of ALL k attempts succeeding. Use when consistency matters (customer support).
Precision@K
Of the top K results shown, how many are relevant? Search/recommendation metric.
Prompt Injection
Adversarial input that overrides the system prompt. The #1 security risk for agents with tool access.
RAG
Retrieval-Augmented Generation: search for docs first, then generate answer using those docs.
Recall@K
Of all relevant items that exist, how many appear in the top K? Search metric.
Red-Teaming
Deliberately trying to break your AI with adversarial inputs. Essential for safety evaluation.
Regression
Quality got worse after a change. The eval equivalent of "you broke it."
ROUGE
Text overlap metric for summarization. Measures word overlap between generated and reference summary.
Rubric
Criteria for judging AI output. Defines dimensions, score levels, and examples for each level.
SEM (Standard Error of Mean)
How much your average score would vary if you re-ran the eval. Smaller = more reliable.
System Prompt
Hidden instructions to the AI that the user never sees. Defines behavior, rules, persona.
Temperature
Controls randomness (0–1). Low = deterministic. High = creative/variable. Affects eval reproducibility.
Token
The unit LLMs process text in. ~4 characters or ~3/4 of a word. Pricing and limits are in tokens.
Trajectory Eval
Evaluating the sequence of steps an agent took, not just the final output. Were the right tools called in a reasonable order?
Win Rate
In A/B comparison, how often does version A beat version B? >60% on 40+ cases is usually significant.
