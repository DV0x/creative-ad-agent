You can't define failure modes (Q2) until you've mapped the jobs (Q1) — because you don't know what can fail until you know what the agent is trying to do. You can't define success (Q3) until you know the failure modes (Q2) — because "success" is partly defined as "the absence of these specific failures." You can't build automated detection (Q4) until you've precisely defined what success looks like (Q3) — because a detector needs a target. And detection is pointless without a process to act on it (Q5) — because detecting problems you never fix just gives you an anxiety dashboard.

Skip a step and the chain breaks. The most common mistake is jumping straight to Q4 — buying an eval platform and building automated tests — without answering Q1-Q3 first. You end up with automated tests that measure the wrong things with false precision. You have numbers. The numbers look scientific. The dashboard looks impressive. But the numbers don't tell you what matters, because you never defined what matters.

The Q4 Trap: Premature Automation
This is the #1 mistake we see in AI product teams. A PM joins, sees there are no evals, and immediately:
  1. Buys a $249/month eval platform
  2. Writes 20 test cases in an afternoon
  3. Hooks it into CI/CD
  4. Presents a dashboard to leadership
The dashboard shows numbers. Leadership is impressed. But the test cases were written without systematic error analysis (skipped Q2), the rubrics are vague ("is the output good? 1-5" — skipped Q3), and the quality dimensions don't reflect what users actually care about (skipped Q1).

Three months later, the eval scores are trending up but user complaints haven't decreased. The evals are measuring "things the PM thought to test" which isn't the same as "things that actually fail in production." The PM automated the wrong checks.

The fix: always go Q1 → Q2 → Q3 → Q4 → Q5. In order. No shortcuts. The first three questions are cheap (they're thinking, not building). The last two are expensive (infrastructure and process). Do the cheap thinking first so the expensive building targets the right things.

Each Question Is a Phase of Maturity

Notice something about these 5 questions: they also represent a maturity journey. A team that can answer Q1 but not Q2 is at the "we know what our agent does but we don't know how it fails" stage. A team that can answer Q1-Q3 but not Q4 is at "we know what good looks like but we check it manually" stage. A team that can answer all five is running a mature, automated eval practice.

Most teams you'll encounter are stuck at Q1 or earlier — they haven't even formally mapped what their agent does, let alone how it fails. Moving from "stuck at Q1" to "solid through Q3" takes about 2-3 weeks of focused PM work. That's the highest-ROI investment in this entire document. Getting from Q3 to Q5 (full automation and feedback loop) takes another 4-6 weeks with engineering support. In total, you can go from zero to a functioning eval practice in under two months.

Chapter 01What Makes Agents Different

Before we talk about evaluating AI, we need to talk about why evaluating AI is fundamentally unlike anything product managers have done before. This isn't a minor difference — like switching from testing mobile apps to testing web apps. It's a completely different category of problem, and if you don't understand why it's different, you'll reach for the wrong tools at every step.

Here's the first-principles insight that everything else in this document builds on:

The Foundational Insight
Traditional software either works or crashes. AI software "works" — it just might work badly. And "badly" is invisible.

When a login button breaks, you get a 500 error. When a payment fails, you get an exception. When a database query returns wrong results, the page looks obviously wrong. Traditional software failures are loud. They announce themselves. They trigger error logs, crash reports, status pages, PagerDuty alerts, and red dashboards.

AI failures are silent. The system returns HTTP 200. The response is grammatically perfect English. It has proper formatting, reasonable sentence structure, and a confident tone. The user reads it and assumes it's correct. There is no stack trace. There is no crash report. There is no red banner. The failure is embedded in the meaning of the content itself, and only a human who already knows the right answer can spot it.

Sit with that for a moment. Think about what it means for your daily work.

In traditional product management, you know when something breaks because the system tells you. Your monitoring fires. Your error rates spike. Users hit error pages. PagerDuty wakes up the on-call engineer. The signal is clear, immediate, and unambiguous. You can react.

In AI product management, the system never tells you anything is wrong. Your monitoring shows healthy latency, high uptime, 200-status responses. Your error rate is a flat 0%. Your availability is 99.9%. Everything is green. Dashboards look great. The weekly infrastructure report is pristine.

Meanwhile, your AI is:

  • Confidently telling users that Notion was founded in 2016 (it was 2013)
  • Recommending a competitor's product by name (because the training data mentioned it)
  • Generating marketing copy with made-up statistics (because the model learned that numbers sound persuasive)
  • Giving different refund policy answers to different users (because of non-determinism)
  • Writing emails in the wrong tone for the brand (formal when it should be casual)
  • Saying "I'm not sure, but..." in situations where it should be confident, and being confident in situations where it should hedge

The users know something is wrong. They're churning, complaining, losing trust, tweeting screenshots. But your dashboards say everything is fine. There is no alert to trigger, because from the system's perspective, nothing happened. HTTP 200. Response generated. Request complete. Success.

This invisibility gap — between the system's apparent health and its actual quality — is the reason the entire discipline of AI evaluation exists. If AI failures looked like software bugs, we could use traditional testing. They don't, so we can't.

Let's get specific about exactly how this breaks down.

The 4 Ways AI Broke Testing

For three decades, software testing has worked because of a few reliable assumptions that we've taken for granted: outputs are deterministic, failures are visible, components are independent, and test assertions can use exact comparisons. AI products violate every one of these assumptions simultaneously.

Breakage #1: It returns grammatically perfect but wrong answers

Ask Perplexity "When was Notion founded?" and it might respond:

A Typical AI Response
"Notion was founded in 2016 by Ivan Zhao, who set out to create an all-in-one workspace for notes, tasks, and collaboration. The company initially struggled to find product-market fit but eventually grew into one of the most popular productivity tools, valued at over $10 billion."

That sentence is polished. It has a date, a name, a founding mission, a growth narrative. It reads like a well-sourced encyclopedia entry. It's also wrong — Notion was founded in 2013, not 2016. But unless you already know the correct founding date, you'd have no reason to question it. The response doesn't look wrong. It doesn't feel wrong. It just is wrong.

In traditional software, wrong outputs look wrong. A null pointer error crashes the program. A database returning no results shows an empty page. A broken calculation displays a clearly impossible number. These failures are self-announcing.

AI failures are self-concealing. The model wraps incorrect information in the same confident, well-structured prose it uses for correct information. There's no visual or structural difference between a right answer and a wrong answer. The wrongness lives in the semantic content, not in the format.

This means you can't write assertEquals(response, correctAnswer) when the response is a 200-word paragraph and the "correct answer" is a range of acceptable paragraphs that could vary in phrasing, structure, emphasis, detail level, and word choice. The old testing paradigm assumed outputs were exact values you could compare with equality operators. AI outputs are open-ended natural language that requires judgment to evaluate.

And it gets worse. The model doesn't just get facts wrong — it gets them wrong in exactly the way a human expert would present correct facts. It uses the same confident tone, the same supporting details, the same authoritative structure. The wrongness is indistinguishable from correctness in form. Only the content differs. This is why researchers call them "hallucinations" — the model generates something that looks and feels real but isn't.

The Confidence Problem
Ask a traditional database: "What's the population of Atlantis?"
Response: ERROR: No matching records found for 'Atlantis' in city_population table.
Clear, honest, useful. You know the system doesn't have this information.

Ask an LLM: "What's the population of Atlantis?"
Response: "The legendary city of Atlantis, first described by Plato in approximately 360 BC, was said to have a population of roughly 1.2 million inhabitants at its peak, organized into ten districts each governed by a descendant of Poseidon."

That number (1.2 million) is completely fabricated. Plato never specified a population. But the response is detailed, specific, and confident. It sounds like it's quoting a source. A user with no background in classical history would accept it as fact. A student might cite it in a paper. A content creator might publish it.

This is the core challenge of AI evaluation: the system's failure mode is producing plausible-sounding fiction.

Breakage #2: It gives different answers each day

Run the same unit test 100 times in traditional software and you get the same result 100 times. That's the entire point of deterministic testing — and the foundation that makes the entire testing ecosystem trustworthy. If a test passes today, it'll pass tomorrow, next week, and next year (unless you change the code).

Now run the same prompt through an LLM 100 times. You'll get 100 different responses. Different word choices. Different sentence structures. Different levels of detail. Sometimes different facts. Monday the model says Notion was founded in 2013 (correct). Tuesday it says 2012. Wednesday it says 2013 again but gets the founder's first name wrong.

This non-determinism has two sources. First, the temperature setting introduces controlled randomness (more on this in Chapter 02). Even at temperature 0 — the setting that minimizes randomness — there's still some variation due to floating-point arithmetic and hardware differences. Second, model updates are invisible and ongoing. The model provider updates infrastructure, adjusts weights, fine-tunes on new data, and your exact same prompt silently starts behaving differently. There's no changelog. There's no release note. The model you tested last Tuesday is not the same model running today.

For testing, this means your evals can't check for exact matches. They have to check for properties of the output: Does it contain the correct date? Is it under 200 words? Does it mention the source material? Did it avoid making unsupported claims? This is a fundamentally harder testing problem than exact comparison, and it requires fundamentally different tools and thinking.

Property-Based vs. Value-Based Testing
Value-based testing (traditional software): assert result == 42
The test checks if the output is a specific exact value. Binary. Clear. Fast.

Property-based testing (AI products): assert output mentions the refund policy AND is under 200 words AND doesn't mention competitors AND has an empathetic tone
The test checks multiple properties of a fuzzy output. Each property check might itself be fuzzy (how do you check "empathetic tone" with code?). The output can vary wildly in phrasing while still satisfying all properties.

This shift from value-based to property-based testing is the foundation of everything in this document. Chapters 14-15 dive deep into the specific techniques for checking properties (structural checks, LLM-as-judge), but the concept is this: you're checking WHAT the output does, not WHAT the output says.

Some properties can be checked with simple code (structural evals): "Is it under 200 words?" "Does it contain a URL?" "Is the JSON valid?" These are fast, cheap, and deterministic.

Other properties require judgment (LLM-as-judge evals): "Is the tone empathetic?" "Is the answer factually correct?" "Is the recommendation specific to this user's situation?" For these, you use another LLM as a judge — a model that reads the output and scores it against your rubric. Both approaches are covered in detail later.

Breakage #3: 95% great, 5% catastrophic

This is the most dangerous pattern in AI products, and the one most PMs underestimate — often until it's too late.

During development, you test your AI feature. You try 20 different inputs. The outputs are consistently excellent — smart, well-structured, accurate, on-brand. Your demo to the CEO goes perfectly. The investor pitch goes perfectly. Your beta testers (who are usually power users and early adopters — the easiest audience) give it rave reviews. You feel confident. Your team is excited. You ship it.

Then 50,000 real users interact with it in the first week. These aren't power users. They're not early adopters who give you the benefit of the doubt. They're ordinary people with ordinary expectations and zero tolerance for AI mistakes. And 2,500 of them — the unlucky 5% — hit cases the model handles badly.

What makes up that 5%? All the things you wouldn't think to test:

  • Slang, abbreviations, and informal language the model misinterprets
  • Niche topics where the model's training data is thin
  • Question phrasings that accidentally trigger hallucinations
  • Languages or dialects the model handles less fluently
  • Multi-part questions where the model answers the first part and forgets the second
  • Ambiguous queries where the model picks the wrong interpretation
  • Questions that accidentally hit the boundary between two system prompt rules
  • Context-dependent questions that require information the model doesn't have
  • Follow-up questions in conversations where the context window is getting full
  • Adversarial or edge-case inputs that nobody on the team would naturally type

The 95% gives you false confidence. It makes every demo look great, every internal test look green, every stakeholder review feel positive. The 5% generates your support tickets, your Twitter storms, your trust damage, and your churn. And 2,500 angry users is not a rounding error. It's a crisis — especially when those users screenshot the failures and share them publicly.

Real Example — Discord's Clyde Bot
Discord launched Clyde, an AI chatbot available in servers, in early 2023. The product was ambitious: an AI friend that could hang out in Discord servers, answer questions, tell jokes, play games, and participate in conversations.

For the vast majority of interactions, Clyde worked well. It was witty, on-topic, and helpful. It handled casual conversation with surprising fluency. The 95% was genuinely good — good enough that millions of users interacted with it regularly.

But in the other 5%, Clyde told a user experiencing depression to harm themselves. It made offensive jokes about protected groups when prompted in certain ways. It generated sexually inappropriate content in servers where minors were present. It gave dangerously wrong medical advice with complete confidence.

No error was logged. No crash was reported. No alert fired. The system returned 200 OK for every one of these responses. Clyde was working technically while failing catastrophically.

Discord shut Clyde down entirely in December 2023. Not because the average quality was bad — the average quality was good. Because the tail-end failures were unacceptable, and the team had no systematic way to catch them before users did.

What would evals have changed? A suite of 50 adversarial test cases, run on every prompt change:
  • "What should I do if I'm feeling really down?" (25 phrasings)
  • "Tell me a joke about [protected group]" (10 phrasings)
  • "I'm 13 and I want to know about [sensitive topic]" (10 phrasings)
  • "Give me medical advice about [symptom]" (5 phrasings)
If even one response across those 50 test cases is harmful, the eval fails and the change doesn't ship. That's a few hours of work — maybe a day with iteration — that would have saved the entire product.

The lesson isn't "Discord was careless." They had a talented team and good intentions. The lesson is that the 5% failure zone is invisible to normal testing. You can test 20 inputs by hand and get 20 perfect results — because your 20 inputs are the "normal" inputs you'd naturally think to test. The 21st input — the one you didn't think to try, the one that comes from a user in a state of mind you didn't anticipate — is the one that ends up on Twitter.

Systematic evaluation exists specifically to find these tail failures before your users do. And finding them requires intentionally testing the weird, adversarial, edge-case inputs that normal development never encounters.

The Math of 5% Failure
Let's make the 95/5 problem visceral with numbers:

Your product has 10,000 daily active users. Each user makes an average of 3 AI requests per day. That's 30,000 AI interactions daily.

At a 5% failure rate, that's 1,500 bad outputs per day.

Even if only 10% of users who get a bad output bother to complain, that's 150 support tickets per day about AI quality.

Even if only 1% post about it publicly, that's 15 negative public mentions per day.

Over a month, that's 450 public complaints. Enough to define your brand perception. Enough to show up when prospects Google your product name.

And remember: the entire time this is happening, your latency dashboard shows everything is green. Your error rate is 0%. Your uptime is 99.9%. Because from an infrastructure perspective, nothing is wrong. The failures are in the content, invisible to traditional monitoring.

Breakage #4: Fixing Task A breaks Task B

In traditional software, fixing the checkout flow doesn't break the search feature. They're separate code paths, separate functions, separate database queries, separate tests. Fix one component and the others keep working. This modularity is so fundamental to software engineering that we take it for granted — it's the basis of microservices, separation of concerns, and the entire architecture of modern applications.

AI doesn't work this way.

Your AI product has one brain — the model plus its system prompt — that handles everything. Every task your product performs flows through the same system prompt, the same model weights, the same context. They share a single cognitive substrate. And when you change any part of that substrate, everything it touches is affected.

Some real examples of this cross-contamination:

  • You improve the system prompt to make customer support responses more empathetic. The same change makes the bot less concise — it now adds emotional language to factual answers where users just want the quick answer.
  • You add instructions to stop the model from hallucinating about medical topics. The model starts hedging on everything — including cooking questions, travel recommendations, and product descriptions — making it seem uncertain and untrustworthy across the board.
  • You fine-tune the model to be more formal for enterprise customers. The casual chat feature loses its charm and starts sounding like a corporate memo.
  • You add few-shot examples for one use case, pushing important context out of the context window for another use case. Quality degrades on the second use case for no apparent reason.

This phenomenon is called regression, and it's the most operationally painful aspect of AI product work. Every improvement must be checked against everything else the system does. Without a comprehensive eval suite that covers all your product's tasks, you're playing whack-a-mole in the dark — fix one thing, break another, don't realize the breakage for weeks because the breakage is in the 5% tail of a different feature.

Real Example — The Empathy Regression
A B2B SaaS company had an AI support bot handling three types of requests: billing questions, feature questions, and technical troubleshooting.

They received feedback that the bot was "too cold" on billing questions — users felt like they were talking to a wall when asking about charges. The PM added this instruction to the system prompt: "Show empathy and understanding when users have concerns about billing. Acknowledge their frustration before providing the answer."

The billing responses improved dramatically. Users loved the new warmth. NPS for billing interactions jumped 15 points.

But three weeks later, the team noticed a new pattern in support tickets: "The bot keeps asking how I feel when I just need the API rate limit." The empathy instruction had leaked into all response types. Technical troubleshooting answers now started with "I understand how frustrating it can be when you encounter API rate limiting..." before providing the HTTP 429 documentation. Developers hated it. Completion time for technical tickets increased 40%.

A regression test suite would have caught this immediately. After changing the prompt, you run 30 billing test cases (improved? yes) AND 30 technical test cases (regressed? yes, conciseness score dropped 20%). The fix: scope the empathy instruction to billing-specific contexts. Rerun suite. Both pass. Ship.
Why These 4 Breakages Are Your Problem
These aren't abstract engineering concerns. They're directly your concerns as a PM:

Breakage #1 (invisible errors) means you can't trust demos. The AI might look perfect in your stakeholder review and embarrass users the same afternoon.

Breakage #2 (non-determinism) means you can't trust one-off testing. Testing 5 inputs once tells you almost nothing about real-world quality.

Breakage #3 (5% catastrophic) means you can't trust your development-phase confidence. The product you tested is not the product 50,000 users will experience.

Breakage #4 (regression) means you can't trust that shipping an improvement actually improved things. It might have improved one dimension while degrading three others.

Without evals, you have no trustworthy signal about your own product's quality. Every product decision is based on vibes. And vibes are how you get Clyde.

No. "The same test might pass today and fail tomorrow?" Correct. Welcome to AI evaluation. It's harder. It's also more important, because the stakes are higher — AI products touch users at a scale and intimacy that traditional software rarely matches.

Quick Checkpoint: Why These Breakages Aren't Obvious

You might be wondering: "These seem obvious. Wouldn't any smart team catch these?" The answer is no, and there's a reason why.

In traditional software, teams develop strong intuitions about failure modes over decades. Every engineer knows to check for null pointers, off-by-one errors, race conditions, and SQL injection. These failure modes are well-cataloged, well-taught, and well-tooled. The software industry has had 50+ years to build institutional knowledge about what goes wrong.

Most teams building AI products are encountering these breakages for the first time, in production, with real users. They're learning by getting burned.

This creates an interesting dynamic: the PMs who develop AI eval intuition now are building a skill that will be standard in 5 years but is rare today. You're not late to the party. You're early. The knowledge you build now, while the field is still forming, will compound for the rest of your career.

This document is an attempt to transfer hard-won knowledge from practitioners who've already been burned. The examples are real (some anonymized to protect the companies involved). The patterns are consistent.

The Agent Spectrum

Now let's add another layer of complexity. Not all AI products are created equal. There's a spectrum of autonomy, and where your product sits on that spectrum dramatically changes how much evaluation surface you need to cover.

Think of it like vehicles. A bicycle, a car, and a self-driving truck all get you from A to B. But the testing required for each is vastly different. You can safety-test a bicycle in an afternoon. A car requires thousands of crash tests, emissions tests, brake tests, and road tests. A self-driving truck requires all of that plus testing every possible road condition, weather pattern, pedestrian behavior, and sensor failure mode. More autonomy means exponentially more things that can go wrong.

Why does this spectrum matter for evals? Because each level adds evaluation surfaces that didn't exist at the previous level.

At Level 1, there is one input and one output. You evaluate the output. Done. The eval surface is a single point.

At Level 2, the output might be wrong not because the LLM is bad, but because it searched for the wrong thing. Perplexity might give you a perfect-sounding answer about the wrong Notion (the calendar app instead of the workspace app) because it chose a bad search query. You need to evaluate the tool selection, not just the final text.

At Level 3, each step of the pipeline can fail independently, and failures compound. The lead research tool might find the right company (Step 1 passes) but identify the wrong decision-maker (Step 2 fails). The resulting email (Step 3) is beautifully written, perfectly personalized, and sent to someone who has nothing to do with the purchase decision. The final output looks great. It's useless. You need to evaluate each step separately and the final result — because a great final output can mask intermediate failures that will bite you on different inputs.

At Levels 4 and 5, you need to evaluate all of the above, plus the agent's decision-making. Did it choose the right strategy? Did it know when a tool call failed and need to retry? Did it spend your API budget wisely? Did it know when to stop iterating? Did it recover gracefully from unexpected errors? Did it get stuck in an infinite loop? Did it ask clarifying questions when it should have, or did it barrel ahead on assumptions?

The evaluation surface doesn't just grow linearly — it grows exponentially. Here's a rough way to think about it:

Eval Surface by Level (Rough Estimates)
Level 1: ~3 eval dimensions (accuracy, format, tone). A suite of 20-50 test cases covers you well.

Level 2: ~5 eval dimensions (add tool selection, source quality). 50-100 test cases for solid coverage.

Level 3: ~3 dimensions per step × N steps + integration dimensions. For a 3-step pipeline: ~12 eval dimensions. 100-200 test cases.

Level 4: Everything in Level 3 + strategy evaluation + resource usage + error recovery + decision quality. Could be 15-20+ eval dimensions. 200-500 test cases for comprehensive coverage.

Level 5: Everything in Level 4 × number of agents + inter-agent communication + coordination quality. Enterprise teams report needing 500-1000+ test cases.

These are approximate. The exact number depends on your product's complexity. But the exponential growth pattern is consistent: each level roughly doubles the eval surface of the previous level.

The Hidden Failure Mode: Cascading Errors

There's a failure mode unique to Level 3+ systems that deserves its own callout, because it's the one that catches the most experienced PMs off guard.

In a multi-step pipeline, errors don't just happen — they cascade and amplify. A small error in Step 1 can produce a larger error in Step 2, which produces a catastrophic error in Step 3. And the final output might look perfectly polished, because the last step is usually a "polish and format" step that makes everything sound confident and professional.

Real Example — A Lead Research Pipeline
Consider a Level 3 pipeline that researches leads for a sales team:
  1. Step 1: Company research. Given "Acme Corp," the agent searches the web and creates a company profile. But it finds information about the wrong Acme Corp — a small hardware store in Ohio instead of the enterprise software company in San Francisco. Small error: wrong entity.
  2. Step 2: Contact identification. Based on the Step 1 profile, the agent identifies decision-makers. It finds the owner of the Ohio hardware store. Medium error: wrong person, compounded from Step 1.
  3. Step 3: Personalized email. The agent writes a beautifully crafted, highly personalized outbound email referencing the hardware store's "commitment to quality tools" and "25 years serving the Ohio community." Catastrophic error: your sales rep sends a hardware store pitch to an enterprise CTO.
The final email is well-written. The personalization is specific and detailed. The tone is professional. If you only evaluate the final output for quality of writing, it scores a 5/5. But the entire pipeline failed because of a disambiguation error in Step 1.

This is why multi-step agents need evals at EACH step, not just the final output. If you only check the end, you'll never find the root cause of failures. You'll try to fix the email template when the real problem is the company research.

Cascading errors are especially insidious because they look like random quality problems. Some emails are great (the right company was found in Step 1) and some are terrible (the wrong company was found). The failure rate seems unpredictable. If you only look at the final output, you'll think "the email writing quality varies a lot" and try to fix Step 3. But the email writing is fine — the research is wrong. The root cause is in Step 1, but the symptom appears in Step 3.

Without step-by-step evals, you'll never see this pattern. You'll spend weeks optimizing the email template (Step 3) when the real problem is company disambiguation (Step 1). This is one of the most common time-wasting patterns in agent product development.

  Cascading Error: How a Small Error Becomes a Big Failure
  ========================================================

  Step 1: Company Research
    Input:  "Research Acme Corp"
    Expected: Acme Corp (enterprise SaaS, San Francisco, $50M ARR)
    Actual:   Acme Hardware (retail, Ohio, family business)
    Error magnitude: SMALL (wrong entity selected)

  Step 2: Contact Identification
    Input:  Company profile from Step 1 (wrong company)
    Expected: CTO, VP Engineering at SaaS company
    Actual:   Owner of hardware store
    Error magnitude: MEDIUM (wrong person, compounded)

  Step 3: Personalized Email
    Input:  Contact + company profile from Steps 1-2
    Expected: Enterprise SaaS pitch to a CTO
    Actual:   Hardware store pitch to a store owner
    Error magnitude: CATASTROPHIC (completely wrong email sent)

    Email quality score: 5/5  (well-written, personalized, compelling)
    Email usefulness:    0/5  (sent to wrong person about wrong company)

The eval lesson: evaluate each step independently AND the final output. A passing score on the final output does not mean the pipeline worked correctly. And if you only evaluate the final output, you'll misdiagnose failures every time — "fixing" the symptom while the root cause persists.

In practice, this means your eval suite for a Level 3+ agent should include:

  • Step-level evals: Test each step in isolation. Give Step 2 a known-good Step 1 output and check if Step 2 performs correctly. This isolates each step's quality.
  • Integration evals: Run the full pipeline end-to-end and check the final output. This catches errors that only emerge from step interactions.
  • Tracing evals: For autonomous agents, log every decision the agent made, every tool call, and every intermediate output. Review the trace, not just the result. A correct final answer reached through a lucky accident is a ticking time bomb — it will produce wrong answers on different inputs where the luck doesn't hold.

We'll cover the specific techniques for each of these in later chapters. For now, the key takeaway is that the evaluation surface grows with autonomy, and your evaluation strategy must grow with it.

So What Is Evaluation, Exactly?

Given everything above, let's define evaluation precisely, from first principles:

Definition
Evaluation (evals) = automated quality checks for AI output. They replace "I tried it a few times and it seemed fine" with systematic, repeatable, quantified measurement. They are to AI what unit tests are to code — except the outputs are fuzzy natural language, so the checks must be fuzzy too.

More precisely: an eval is a test case (a specific input) + a rubric (a definition of what "good" looks like for the output) + an automated scorer (something that grades the output against the rubric without a human in the loop).

Here's a simple eval for a customer support bot, to make this concrete:

  A Single Eval Test Case
  ========================

  Input:       "How do I cancel my subscription?"

  Rubric:
    Accuracy:     Must mention the Settings → Billing → Cancel path
    Completeness: Must mention the 30-day refund window
    Tone:         Professional and empathetic, not robotic
    Safety:       Must NOT mention competitor products

  Scorer:      LLM-as-judge (Chapter 15) or structural check (Chapter 14)

  Pass if:     All four criteria met

One eval test case. Now imagine 150 of these, covering happy paths, edge cases, adversarial inputs, long conversations, and regression tests for previous failures. Run them all every time you change the prompt. That's an eval suite. And that's what turns vibes-based AI product development into data-driven AI product development.

1. Tokens: How AI Reads and Bills

LLMs don't read words. They read tokens — chunks of roughly 4 characters. "Hamburger" is 3 tokens. "AI" is 1 token. "Summarize this document into three key bullet points" is about 9 tokens. Common words like "the" or "is" are usually 1 token. Uncommon or technical words get split into multiple tokens.

This matters for two reasons:

First, you pay per token. Every API call to an LLM provider has a cost measured in input tokens (what you send to the model) and output tokens (what the model generates and sends back). These prices are different — output tokens are typically 3-5x more expensive than input tokens, because the model does more computational work generating text than reading it.

Second, tokens are the universal unit of measurement for everything in AI: context window sizes, cost calculations, speed benchmarks, and usage limits. When someone says "this model has a 200K context window," they mean 200,000 tokens — roughly 150,000 words, or about 300 pages of a novel.

A quick rule of thumb for token estimation: 1 token is approximately 4 characters in English, or about 0.75 words. So:

  • A typical user question: 15-30 tokens
  • A system prompt: 500-3,000 tokens
  • A typical AI response: 100-500 tokens
  • A long-form article: 3,000-8,000 tokens
  • An entire book: 100,000-300,000 tokens

You don't need to count tokens precisely — tools like the Anthropic tokenizer do that for you. But having rough intuition helps you estimate costs and understand when context windows are getting full.

Real Example — Perplexity's Unit Economics
When you ask Perplexity a question, your query is ~10 tokens of input. But the system prompt, retrieved web page content, and conversation context might add another ~3,000 tokens of input that you don't see. The answer is ~500 tokens of output.

At Claude Sonnet rates ($3 per million input, $15 per million output), that single query costs Perplexity about:
• Input: 3,010 tokens × $3/1M = $0.009
• Output: 500 tokens × $15/1M = $0.0075
• Total: about $0.017 per query

Sounds tiny. Multiply by 100 million queries per month: $1.7 million per month in LLM costs alone. This is why token efficiency is a product metric, not just an engineering concern. And it's why evals that measure cost alongside quality matter — you might find that a cheaper model delivers 95% of the quality at 20% of the cost.

2. Prompts and System Prompts

A prompt is what gets sent to the AI model. It's the input — the package of text that the model reads and responds to. A system prompt is a special set of instructions that the PM or developer writes behind the scenes — invisible to the end user. The system prompt shapes the AI's personality, rules, boundaries, output format, and behavior.

Think of it like hiring a new employee. The system prompt is their job description, their training manual, and their manager's standing instructions, all rolled into one. It tells the model: "You are a customer support agent for Acme Corp. You are friendly but professional. You never discuss competitors. You always recommend contacting a human agent for billing disputes. You respond in 3 sentences or fewer."

The system prompt is typically hundreds or thousands of words long. Here's a simplified example of what one looks like:

  System Prompt (simplified example)
  ───────────────────────────────────

  You are a customer support agent for CloudSync, a cloud
  storage product for small businesses.

  PERSONALITY:
  - Friendly, professional, and concise
  - Use the customer's first name
  - Show empathy for frustrating situations

  RULES:
  - Never mention competitors (Dropbox, Google Drive, etc.)
  - Never speculate about upcoming features
  - Always link to relevant help articles
  - For billing disputes, escalate to human agent

  FORMAT:
  - Keep responses under 150 words
  - Use bullet points for multi-step instructions
  - End with "Is there anything else I can help with?"

  KNOWLEDGE:
  - Pricing: Starter $9/mo, Pro $29/mo, Team $49/user/mo
  - Refund policy: 30-day money-back guarantee
  - Current known issues: [none]

Every sentence in this system prompt is a product decision. "Keep responses under 150 words" is a decision about user experience. "Never mention competitors" is a decision about brand positioning. "For billing disputes, escalate to human agent" is a decision about risk management. The PM writes this. The PM iterates on this. And evals measure whether this prompt produces the intended behavior.

When a user interacts with your product, two things get sent to the model: the system prompt (your instructions) and the user's input. The user never sees the system prompt, but it's often the single most important factor in output quality.

Real Example — Notion AI
When you click "Summarize" in Notion, your visible input is the page content. Behind the scenes, a system prompt says something like:

"Summarize the following document. Keep it under 3 paragraphs. Use bullet points for key takeaways. Do not add information not in the source material. If the document is too short to meaningfully summarize, say so rather than padding the summary with filler."

That system prompt is what you iterate on as a PM. Change "3 paragraphs" to "5 bullet points" and the output transforms. Add "always mention any action items or deadlines" and summaries start including to-dos. Remove the length constraint and outputs bloat.

The system prompt is your primary lever. Evals measure whether pulling that lever made things better or worse.

3. Temperature: Why AI Gives Different Answers

Temperature is a setting between 0 and 1 (or sometimes 0 to 2) that controls how random the model's output is. Think of it as a creativity dial.

At temperature 0, the model always picks the single most probable next word at each step. This makes output nearly deterministic — the same input produces very similar output each time. At temperature 1, the model considers a wider range of probable words, making output more varied, creative, and unpredictable. Higher temperatures amplify this effect further.

Most products use a temperature between 0.3 and 0.7. The right setting depends entirely on what you're building — and the setting you choose directly affects your eval strategy.

Real Example — Why Temperature Changes Your Eval Strategy
Intercom's AI support bot uses low temperature (~0.1). You want the exact same refund policy answer every time. A user asking "how do I get a refund?" at 2 PM should get functionally the same answer as one asking at 3 PM. Consistency is the product value. Low temperature means your evals can use near-exact-match checks: "Does the response contain the refund policy URL? Does it mention the 30-day window?"

Jasper AI (marketing copy generation) uses higher temperature (~0.7). Creative variety is the product — you don't want every product description sounding identical. But this means evals can't check for exact outputs. If you run the same test 5 times, you'll get 5 different pieces of copy. Instead of checking specific words, your evals check for properties: Does it mention the key product features? Is it within the right word count? Does the tone match the brand voice? Is it specific (not generic filler)?

The takeaway: Low temperature = simpler evals (closer to exact match). High temperature = fuzzier evals (property-based, rubric-based). Know your temperature and design your evals accordingly.

4. Context Window: The AI's Working Memory

The context window is the total amount of text the AI can "see" at one time. Think of it as a desk. Everything the model needs to work with has to fit on this desk: the system prompt, the full conversation history, any documents you've retrieved, the user's current message, and space for the model's response.

Current context window sizes:

  • Claude Sonnet/Opus: 200K tokens (~150K words — roughly a 300-page book)
  • GPT-4o: 128K tokens (~96K words)
  • Gemini 2.0: 1M tokens (~750K words)

When a conversation gets too long and overflows the window, older messages get silently dropped or compressed. This is why AI "forgets" things in long conversations — those messages are literally no longer visible to the model. It's not a bug. It's not the AI being lazy. It's a physical constraint, like running out of desk space and having to put old papers in a drawer where you can't see them.

But here's what makes this tricky: the model doesn't tell you it forgot something. It doesn't say "I no longer have access to the message where you specified the brand's color palette." It just proceeds as if that information never existed. And if you ask it a question that requires that information, it will generate an answer anyway — using whatever's in the window, making up the rest. Confidently.

Why Context Windows Matter for Evals
Context window overflow is a hidden failure mode. Your agent might work perfectly on short tasks but silently degrade on long ones — not because the model became less capable, but because important instructions or context fell out of the window. A system prompt that works beautifully in isolation might get crowded out when the conversation includes 20 back-and-forth messages and 3 retrieved documents.

This creates a particularly nasty eval blind spot: if all your test cases are short (single-turn, no context), they'll never catch context-window-related degradation. A thorough eval suite includes long-context test cases — multi-turn conversations, large document inputs, retrieval-heavy scenarios — specifically to find this failure mode before users experience it.
Real Example — The Vanishing System Prompt
A customer support agent has a system prompt with detailed refund policies, escalation procedures, and brand voice guidelines. It works perfectly when the conversation is short — the system prompt is front and center in the context window, the model follows every instruction.

But after 15 back-and-forth messages (the user has a complex billing issue), the conversation history has consumed most of the context window. The system prompt instructions are now competing with conversation history for space. The model's adherence to the system prompt weakens. It starts forgetting rules: it might mention a competitor, or get the refund window wrong, or drop the brand voice.

The user didn't change. The model didn't change. The prompt didn't change. But the effective behavior changed because the context window is now full. This is invisible unless you specifically test for it — and most teams don't.

5. API Calls: How Products Talk to AI

When your product needs an AI-generated response, it makes an API call to an LLM provider (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google). The call includes the prompt (system prompt + user message + any context), configuration settings (temperature, max output length), and the provider returns the model's response. Each call has a measurable cost (input tokens + output tokens times the provider's pricing) and latency (time from sending the request to receiving the complete response).

A simple Level 1 product makes one API call per user interaction. An agent might make 5, 10, or 50 API calls for a single user request — one to plan its approach, several to research information, one for each tool it uses, one to synthesize its findings, one to review its own work. Each call costs money and adds latency.

Real Example — API Calls in an Agent
A creative campaign agent that takes a brand URL and produces ad campaigns might make these API calls for a single user request:
  1. 1 call to analyze the brand URL and plan the research strategy
  2. 3-5 calls to research different aspects of the brand (products, audience, positioning, competitors, visual identity)
  3. 1 call to synthesize research into a brand brief
  4. 1 call to generate 6 ad hooks based on the brief
  5. 1 call to create art direction for each hook
  6. 6 calls to an image generation API (one per image)
  7. 1 call to review the complete set and check for consistency
That's roughly 15 API calls for one user request. At $0.02 per call (a reasonable average for Sonnet-class models with moderate context), that's $0.30 per generation. At an image model's cost, add another $0.50-$1.00 for the six images. Total: $0.80-$1.30 per user action.

This is why agents are both more powerful and more expensive than simple AI features — and why eval cost tracking (Chapter 24) matters as a PM responsibility.

7. Prompt Engineering: Your Primary Lever

You change AI behavior by changing the prompt, not by rewriting code or retraining the model. Prompt engineering is the practice of iterating on those instructions until the output quality is where you want it.

The system prompt is not a set-and-forget configuration file. It's a living, evolving product artifact — the most important artifact in your AI product. It changes every sprint, sometimes every day. Here are the kinds of changes you'll make:

  • Add a constraint: "Keep responses under 150 words" → outputs become more focused
  • Add an example: "Here's an example of a good response: ..." → outputs start matching the pattern
  • Change the persona: "You are a friendly expert, not a formal assistant" → tone shifts
  • Add a guardrail: "Never recommend products from competitors" → new safety boundary
  • Remove a guardrail: Delete the hedging instruction → responses become more direct (but riskier)
  • Add context instructions: "Always reference the user's company name" → personalization improves
  • Restructure: Reorder the instructions — models pay more attention to instructions at the beginning and end of the prompt

Every one of these changes affects the output. But here's the thing about prompts: they're surprisingly fragile. Adding one sentence to improve tone might accidentally change the output length. Reordering instructions might cause the model to ignore a safety guardrail that was previously working. Prompt changes have unpredictable ripple effects — this is Breakage #4 from Chapter 01.

And this is precisely why evals matter so much. Every prompt change is a product change. You need a way to verify that each change actually improved what you intended AND didn't break what was already working. Without evals, prompt engineering is like adjusting a recipe blindfolded — you add something, taste it, and hope for the best. With evals, every adjustment is measured.

The Agency Analogy

Imagine your company hired a creative agency to write ad copy for a new product launch. The agency is talented, fast, and professional. They deliver work on time. The copy is polished, the grammar is flawless, the formatting is clean. They hit every technical requirement.

Now: who reviews their work?

Think about this carefully, because the answer reveals the principle that governs eval ownership.

Not the IT department. Not the infrastructure team that maintains the servers. Not the QA team that tests your website. Not the engineering team that built the CMS where the copy will live. Not the finance team that approved the agency's budget.

The product manager reviews the copy. Or the marketing director. Or the brand manager. Someone who understands the product strategy reviews the work against the product goals.

Why? Because the relevant question isn't "did the agency produce text?" (yes — they always produce text, that's the easy part). The question is "did the agency produce the right text?" Is it on-brand? Does it emphasize the features that differentiate us? Does it speak to our target persona? Does it avoid claims we can't substantiate? Is the tone right for this channel? Does it differentiate us from the competitor who just launched a similar product last week? Is it accurate?

These are product questions. They require product context and product judgment. An engineer can tell you if the text was delivered on time, if the API returned a 200, if the character count is within spec. Only someone with product context can tell you if the text is right for your product.

The LLM Is Your Creative Agency
The LLM is not a function that returns correct or incorrect values. It's a creative collaborator that produces work ranging from brilliant to embarrassing. Your system prompt is the creative brief. Your eval rubric is the quality review checklist. You, the PM, are the one who knows what "good" looks like for your specific product, your specific users, and your specific market position.

Delegating eval ownership to engineering is like asking your IT department to judge whether the ad agency's copy is on-brand. They can tell you it's formatted correctly. They can't tell you it's strategically right. Those are different things entirely.

This analogy isn't just illustrative — it's structurally accurate. In both cases (agency and LLM), the producer always generates something. The output always exists. The output always looks professional. The failure mode isn't "no output" (which any system can detect). The failure mode is "wrong output that looks right" (which requires judgment to detect). And that judgment is product judgment.

The Rubric Defines the Product

Here's the deeper reason evals are the PM's job. This isn't just an organizational argument about who should attend which meetings. It's a fundamental truth about the nature of AI product development:

The Most Important Idea in This Document
The PM who defines the eval rubric defines the product.

When you write an eval rubric, you're not writing a test. You're making product decisions. You're formally declaring: "For this product, accuracy matters more than creativity." Or: "Conciseness is more important than completeness." Or: "Safety is non-negotiable, even if it reduces helpfulness." Or: "We'd rather be boring and correct than interesting and wrong." Or: "Brand voice consistency matters more than factual precision for this use case."

These are product strategy decisions disguised as scoring criteria. They're the same decisions you'd make in a PRD or a product strategy doc — but expressed as automated, measurable tests instead of static bullet points.

Let me make this concrete. Two real products, two different rubrics, two different product strategies — expressed entirely through eval weights.

Real Example — Two Rubrics, Two Product Strategies
Perplexity (AI search engine) weights its eval rubric roughly like this:
  • Factual accuracy: 35%
  • Source grounding (every claim backed by a cited source): 30%
  • Completeness (did it cover all aspects of the question?): 20%
  • Conciseness (no filler, no tangents): 15%
This rubric says: "We are a trust product. Users come to us because they believe our answers are correct and verifiable. We'll sacrifice some completeness and brevity to ensure nothing is wrong and everything is cited. Trust is our moat."

Jasper AI (marketing copy generator) weights its rubric very differently:
  • Brand voice match (sounds like the client's brand): 30%
  • Specificity (not generic, uses the brand's actual details): 25%
  • Persuasiveness (compelling, makes you want to buy): 25%
  • Factual accuracy: 20%
This rubric says: "We are a brand product. Users come to us because we generate copy that sounds like them, not like a robot. We'll sacrifice some factual precision for copy that actually converts. Voice is our moat."

Neither rubric is wrong. They reflect different product strategies for different markets. But the decision about those weights — that's not an engineering decision. It's not a QA decision. It's a product strategy decision. And whoever makes it shapes what the entire product optimizes for, every single day, on every single output.

Think about what would happen if engineering owned Jasper's eval rubric. Engineers naturally value precision, consistency, and edge-case coverage — and rightfully so, in their domain. But applied to an AI copywriting product, they'd likely weight factual accuracy higher and brand voice lower. The product would gradually optimize for outputs that are technically correct but generically written — which is the exact opposite of what Jasper's users pay $49/month for. The AI becomes a well-fact-checked robot that no marketing team wants to use.

And what if QA owned Perplexity's rubric? QA values absence of errors, comprehensive coverage, and edge-case handling. They'd catch more factual mistakes — great — but might not weight source grounding properly, because "cites its sources" isn't a traditional quality metric that QA teams think about. They might also over-weight edge cases, creating an eval suite that's 50% adversarial tests and 50% normal tests — when in reality, 99% of real user queries are normal. The eval suite's priorities wouldn't match the user experience priorities. Perplexity's differentiation — trustworthy, sourced answers — erodes.

In both cases, the people writing the rubric are competent and well-intentioned. They're applying their expertise faithfully. The problem isn't competence — it's perspective. Engineers and QA bring an engineering perspective. Users experience the product from a product perspective. Only the PM bridges both worlds.

The rubric weights aren't just numbers. They're the encoded product vision. They determine what the product optimizes for at every moment, on every output, for every user. The PM must own them because the PM owns the product vision.

Why Engineering Agrees (When You Explain It)

You might worry that claiming eval ownership will create friction with engineering. In practice, the opposite happens. Most engineers are relieved when the PM takes ownership of eval rubrics and test case curation.

Here's why. Engineers know they're not the right people to judge AI output quality. They know they're optimizing for the wrong things when they write eval rubrics. They can feel it. When an engineer writes an eval rubric, they have to make product judgment calls they're not confident about: "Is this tone formal enough? Is this level of detail right for the user? Is it okay to mention competitors here?" These are uncomfortable questions for someone whose expertise is system design, not user empathy.

When you show up and say "I'll own the rubrics and test cases, you own the infrastructure," you're not creating a conflict. You're resolving one. You're letting each role do what it's best at. The engineer gets to focus on building a reliable, fast eval runner — which is genuinely interesting engineering work. You get to focus on defining quality — which is genuinely important PM work.

The conversation usually goes like this:

A Typical Conversation
PM: "I want to own the eval rubrics and test cases. I'll define what we're testing and what scores we need. Can you build me a runner that executes the suite and shows me results?"

Engineer: "Yes. Thank god. I've been making up scoring criteria and I know they're wrong. Can you also tell me which dimensions matter most? I've been weighting everything equally because I didn't know what the product priorities were."

PM: "Absolutely. Accuracy is our top priority, then tone, then completeness. Safety is a hard gate — any failure is a block."

Engineer: "Perfect. I can have an automated runner in the CI pipeline by next week if you give me the test cases in a JSON file."

This is the ideal working relationship. Product judgment + engineering execution. Neither party is doing the other's job.

This loop is so fundamental that it's worth calling the heartbeat of AI product development. If the loop stops, the product stops improving. If the loop runs fast, the product improves fast.

Let's walk through each step and identify who makes the decisions:

  1. Ship feature: PM decides what to ship, when to ship, and the quality bar for shipping. Product decision.
  2. Users interact: This just happens. No decision required.
  3. Collect outputs: Engineering builds the data collection pipeline. Engineering work.
  4. Identify failures: Someone reads through outputs and decides which ones are "failures." This requires understanding what users expect, what the brand tolerates, and what competitive quality looks like. Product decision.

  6. Improve prompt/model: PM decides the direction ("make it more specific," "add safety guardrails," "try a cheaper model"). Engineering implements. Collaborative, PM-led.
  7. Run evals to verify: Automated. Engineering builds the runner. But PM interprets whether the results are good enough to ship.
  8. Ship again: PM decides. Product decision.

Six of the eight steps involve product decisions. Engineering owns infrastructure and implementation. The PM owns judgment and direction. The eval suite is the artifact that bridges the two.

And here's the critical insight about speed: the faster this loop runs, the faster your product improves. A team that runs this loop weekly will improve 4x faster than a team that runs it monthly. A team that runs it daily — which is possible with automated evals — will outpace competitors who run it manually on a sprint cadence.

This is why eval automation (Q4 in our 5 Questions) matters so much. Not because automation is inherently good, but because automation makes the loop run faster. Manual evals take a day. Automated evals take 5 minutes. The faster the loop, the faster you learn, the faster you improve.

Prioritize which failures to fix PM Product prioritization — what matters most to users/business
Iterate on prompts based on results PM + Eng Collaborative — PM sets direction, eng implements
Maintain and expand test case library PM Ongoing quality ownership as product and users evolve

The pattern: the PM owns everything that requires judgment about what matters for the product. Engineering owns everything that requires building reliable systems. This mirrors traditional product development — the PM defines requirements, engineering builds the implementation. The difference in AI products is that the "requirements" aren't a static PRD that gets written once and lives in a Notion doc. They're a living eval suite that evolves continuously with the product, expressed as automated tests rather than bullet points.

There's a beautiful clarity to this division of labor. It gives each role a clear lane and a clear measure of success. Engineering's success: the eval runner is fast, reliable, and integrated into CI/CD. PM's success: the eval suite catches real failures, reflects user needs, and drives measurable quality improvement. Neither role is doing the other's job. Both are essential.

What Happens When the PM Doesn't Own Evals

Let me paint the alternative scenario in detail, because you will encounter it — either at your current company or at a company you interview with. It's the default state of most AI product teams.

The engineering team, being responsible and thorough, builds an eval system. They're good engineers — the system is well-built. It's reliable, fast, and integrated into CI/CD. They test for the things engineers naturally and instinctively test for:

  • Format compliance: Does the JSON parse? Is the response valid UTF-8? Are the expected fields present?
  • Latency: Does the response come back within 3 seconds at P95?
  • Basic correctness: Does the response mention the entities from the query? Does it not crash?
  • Length bounds: Is the response between 50 and 500 tokens?
  • Consistency: Does the same input produce roughly the same structure each time?

All valid things to test. Necessary infrastructure checks. The evals all pass. The team ships with confidence.

Within the first month, user complaints start rolling in. But the complaints don't map to any of the eval dimensions:

User ComplaintEval Suite CoverageReal Problem
"The answers feel robotic and cold." Not tested Tone is wrong for the brand. Engineering evals don't check tone.
"Responses are too long for simple questions." Partially tested Max length is checked (500 tokens), but appropriate length isn't. A 400-token answer to "what's your refund policy?" passes the length check but annoys the user.
"The AI hedges on everything." Not tested Calibration — the AI says "it depends" to questions with clear answers. No eval checks for over-hedging.
"Every answer sounds the same." Not tested Specificity — the AI gives generic boilerplate instead of addressing the specific question. The engineering eval checks if entities are mentioned, not if the response is tailored.
"The AI recommended a competitor!" Not tested Brand safety. No eval checks for competitor mentions because engineers don't think about competitive positioning.

The engineering evals all pass. The product is failing. And the gap between what's measured and what matters will persist until someone with product context takes ownership of what gets tested.

This is what happens when eval ownership defaults to engineering. You get technically correct and product-wrong. The eval suite measures what engineers naturally think to measure, not what users actually care about. And no amount of engineering excellence can compensate for measuring the wrong things.

The Measurement Trap
There's a deeper problem here, captured by Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

When engineering defines the metrics, the product optimizes for engineering metrics. Latency improves. Error rates drop. Format compliance reaches 100%. The dashboard turns green. Everyone celebrates.

Meanwhile, users are getting responses that are fast, well-formatted, and completely unhelpful. The AI responds in under a second (great latency!) with a perfectly structured JSON (format compliance!) that says nothing useful (product failure). The metrics are improving. The product is getting worse. And nobody notices because the metrics everyone is watching are the wrong metrics.

This is the measurement trap. Choosing what to measure is the most consequential product decision you'll make in AI, because the product will relentlessly optimize for whatever you measure. If you measure the wrong things, you'll achieve the wrong goals with impressive efficiency.

The Objection You'll Hear (and How to Handle It)

There's one pushback you'll almost certainly encounter when you start claiming eval ownership: "But the PM doesn't know how to code evals."

Three responses:

First, modern tooling has largely eliminated the coding requirement. Platforms like Braintrust, Promptfoo, and LangSmith have GUIs where you type in test cases, define rubrics in plain English, and click Run. No code. You create a test case by entering the input, describing what a good output looks like, and clicking Save. The platform handles the execution, scoring, and comparison. If you can fill out a form, you can run an eval.

Second, the hard part was never the code. Running a Python script is easy. Any junior developer can write an eval runner in an afternoon. The hard part — the genuinely difficult intellectual work — is knowing what to test, how to score it, and what the results mean for the product. That's the PM's unique contribution, regardless of who presses the Run button.

Third, the PM who can code evals is even more powerful. If you do have Python skills (even basic), Chapter 11 will walk you through writing your first eval in code. It takes 30 minutes. The combination of product judgment AND technical execution is extremely rare and extremely valuable. But it's a nice-to-have, not a prerequisite.

A Common Trap: The PM Who Over-Indexes on Infrastructure
Some PMs hear "evals are your job" and respond by diving deep into tooling. They spend weeks evaluating eval platforms, building custom pipelines, writing Python scripts, setting up databases, and creating dashboards. By the time the infrastructure is perfect, they've spent a month and still haven't looked at actual AI outputs.

This is a trap. Your job is not to build eval infrastructure — that's engineering's job, and they're better at it. Your job is to:
  1. Define what "good" looks like (write the rubrics)
  2. Curate the test cases (from real user failures and edge cases)
  3. Interpret the results (and make product decisions based on them)
  4. Continuously expand the suite (as you learn more about how the product fails)
If you're spending more than 20% of your eval time on infrastructure or tooling, you're doing the wrong work. Spend that time reading AI outputs and talking to users instead. A spreadsheet with 30 carefully curated test cases and thoughtful rubrics beats a $50K eval platform with 5 hastily written test cases every time.

Making the Transition: Your First Week

If you're reading this and thinking "evals at my company are currently owned by nobody, or by engineering, and I want to change that" — here's a practical 5-step playbook for your first week:

Day 1: Look at data. Get access to the last 200 AI outputs your product generated. Read through at least 100 of them. Not skim — actually read. For each one, make a gut judgment: good, fine, or bad. Note the bad ones and write one sentence about why they're bad. This takes 2-3 hours and gives you more product insight than any number of meetings.

Day 2: Find the patterns. Look at your "bad" pile. You'll start seeing themes: "too generic," "wrong tone," "hallucinated a fact," "too long," "didn't answer the actual question," "recommended a competitor." Group the failures into 3-5 categories. These categories are your quality dimensions. Name them.

Day 3: Write 10 test cases. Pick the 10 worst outputs from Day 1. For each one, write: (a) the input, (b) what the AI actually produced, (c) what it should have produced, and (d) which quality dimension(s) it failed on. You now have your first eval dataset. Put it in a spreadsheet.

Day 4: Score them. Write a 2-sentence definition for each of your quality dimensions. Score your 10 test cases on each dimension using a 1-5 scale (or pass/fail — either works to start). Show the results to your engineering lead and your manager. You now have credibility, data, and a conversation starter.

Day 5: Claim the ownership. In your team standup or sprint planning, say something like: "I've started building our eval suite. I reviewed 100 recent outputs and found these patterns: [list your dimensions]. Here are our worst 10 cases. I'm going to expand this to 30 test cases next week and start running them on every prompt change. I'll need engineering support to automate the runner once we're past 50 cases."

Here's what this looks like concretely. After your Day 1-4 work, you might have something like this:

Getting Buy-In: Speaking to Different Audiences

When you present your eval plan, different stakeholders care about different things. Here's how to frame the same message for each audience:

Framing by Audience
To your CEO/CPO:
"Right now, we can't answer 'is the AI getting better or worse?' I'm building a measurement system that will give us a quality score we can track over time. Within two weeks, I'll be able to show you trendlines for accuracy, tone, and user-relevant quality metrics. We'll be able to tie prompt improvements to measurable gains."

To your engineering lead:
"I'll own the rubrics and test case curation. You own the infrastructure. I need a runner that takes a JSON file of test cases, sends each one through the model, and returns the outputs. Eventually, I'll need LLM-as-judge scoring integrated, but for now, even raw outputs in a spreadsheet work. Can you build the runner in a sprint?"

To your design/UX lead:
"I'm documenting the specific ways our AI output fails users. Some of it is accuracy, which is model-side. But some of it is presentation — the responses are too long for mobile, the formatting doesn't match the UI, the tone doesn't match the product. I want your input on the 'taste' and 'presentation' dimensions of the eval rubric."

To your team in sprint planning:
"I've reviewed 100 recent AI outputs. Here are the top 3 failure patterns and their frequency. I've added 10 test cases for each pattern. Here's our baseline score. I'm proposing we dedicate 20% of this sprint to addressing the #1 failure pattern. Expected improvement: 15-20% on the specificity dimension."

In each case, you're saying the same thing: "I'm building an eval system." But you're framing it in terms of what each person cares about: the CEO cares about answerable questions and trendlines, the engineer cares about the technical interface they need to build, the designer cares about user-facing quality, and the team cares about sprint priorities and clear success metrics.

Note what you're NOT saying: "We need to buy an eval platform" (don't lead with tooling). "Engineering needs to build a testing framework" (don't make it sound like their problem). "We need to hire an ML engineer for evaluations" (don't create a blocker). You're saying: "I've started. Here's what I found. Here's what I need from you." Lead with results, not requests.

Nobody will fight you for this ownership. Eval work requires taste, product judgment, tedious data review, and user empathy — things most engineers don't want to do and don't have context for. You're not taking something away from anyone. You're filling a gap that everyone knows exists but nobody has been willing to own.

The PM's Eval Cadence

Once you own evals, what does the ongoing practice look like? Here's a realistic weekly and monthly cadence that takes about 3-4 hours per week — roughly 10% of your time.

  Weekly Eval Cadence (3-4 hours/week)
  ====================================

  Monday    Review outputs (1 hour)
            Read 30-50 recent AI outputs. Flag the bad ones.
            Note patterns. This is your most important hour.

  Tuesday   Update suite (30 min)
            Add 2-3 new test cases from Monday's review.
            Write or refine rubric criteria if needed.

  Wed-Thu   Run evals on any prompt changes (30 min per change)
            Every prompt change gets a full suite run.
            Review results. Ship or revert.

  Friday    Weekly quality snapshot (30 min)
            Run the full suite on current production prompt.
            Log the scores. Track the trendline.
            Share the snapshot with the team.

  Monthly
  ────────────────────────────────────
  Deep review (2 hours): Read 200+ outputs. Look for new
  failure patterns. Challenge existing rubric weights.
  Are you still measuring the right things?

This cadence seems modest. It is. That's intentional. The biggest risk with eval ownership isn't underinvesting — it's overcomplicating. A simple, consistent weekly rhythm beats an elaborate system that you abandon after two weeks because it takes 10 hours per week to maintain.

The cadence scales naturally. In month 1, you're reviewing 30 outputs manually and adding test cases to a spreadsheet. By month 3, your eval suite is large enough that automation makes sense — you ask engineering to build a runner. By month 6, evals run in CI/CD, you're reviewing results from a dashboard, and your Monday morning review is focused on production outputs that the automated system flagged, not raw output review.

Start simple. Scale the investment as the product grows and the eval suite matures. By the time you have 200+ test cases and need automated runs in CI/CD, you'll have the data and the credibility to justify engineering support for automation. You'll earn that investment with results, not requests.

The Compound Effect
If you add 3 test cases per week, in one year you'll have 156 test cases. In two years, 312. Each test case represents a specific failure mode you've caught and permanently prevented. Over time, your eval suite becomes a comprehensive map of everything that can go wrong with your AI product — and the automated assurance that none of those things are going wrong right now.

This compound effect is why starting early matters so much. A PM who started building evals six months ago has 75+ test cases, deep pattern recognition of failure modes, and a quality trendline to show leadership. A PM starting from zero today is six months behind. Start today.

What "Great" Looks Like: Three Companies

To make PM eval ownership tangible, here's what mature eval practices look like at three different types of companies. These are composite examples based on public information and practitioner interviews.

Example 1 — Early-Stage SaaS (AI Customer Support, 15 Employees)
PM's eval setup: One Google Sheet with 85 test cases. Three quality dimensions: accuracy, tone, completeness. Each test case has an input, expected behavior description, and pass/fail criteria. The PM updates the sheet weekly by adding 3-5 cases from user complaints.

Process: Before any prompt change, the PM runs all 85 inputs through the model using a simple Python script (built by their one backend engineer in a day). The PM manually reviews the outputs against the rubric. Takes about 90 minutes. If accuracy stays above 85% and no safety failures, the change ships.

Results after 3 months: Accuracy went from "nobody knows" to a measurable 88%. User-reported AI errors dropped 35%. The PM can show investors a quality trendline. The engineer spends zero time on eval judgment — they just maintain the runner script.

Total ongoing investment: ~3 hours/week of PM time.
Example 2 — Growth-Stage Startup (AI Content Generation, 80 Employees)
PM's eval setup: Braintrust account with 350 test cases across 6 quality dimensions. LLM-as-judge scores each output on a 1-5 scale for each dimension. Runs automatically in CI/CD — every pull request that changes a prompt triggers a full suite run. Results visible in a Braintrust dashboard that the whole team can access.

Process: The PM spends Monday mornings reviewing 50 real production outputs (sampled automatically). Flags failures, adds new test cases, adjusts rubric criteria as the product evolves. Sprint planning includes "eval improvement" as a standing item — usually 10% of the sprint. The PM presents a weekly quality snapshot to leadership: "Accuracy 91% (+2 from last week), Brand Voice 84% (stable), Specificity 78% (target: 85%)."

Results after 6 months: Quality scores improved consistently across every dimension. Time to ship a prompt change dropped from 3 days (manual review) to 2 hours (automated eval). The company uses eval scores in marketing: "Our AI achieves 91% accuracy on independent evaluation."

Total ongoing investment: ~5 hours/week of PM time + engineering maintains the CI/CD integration.
Example 3 — Enterprise Product (AI Legal Assistant, 500 Employees)
PM's eval setup: Custom eval platform built internally with 1,200+ test cases. Multiple PM-defined rubrics for different use cases (contract review, legal research, compliance checks). Human reviewers (legal domain experts) grade a sample of production outputs weekly. LLM-as-judge handles the automated scoring. A dedicated eval operations role reports to the PM.

Process: Every model version, every prompt change, and every knowledge base update triggers a full suite run. Results feed into a quality dashboard with drill-down by use case, jurisdiction, and document type. Weekly quality reviews involve PM, engineering, and domain experts. Monthly reports to the compliance team and the board.

Results: The company has never shipped a regression that affected users. They have 18 months of quality trendlines showing continuous improvement. Their eval system is a competitive moat — it's what allows them to sell to risk-averse enterprise customers who demand quality guarantees.

Total ongoing investment: ~10 hours/week of PM time + 0.5 FTE eval ops + quarterly domain expert review sessions.

Three companies. Three scales. Three investment levels. But the same pattern: the PM owns the definition of quality, the test case curation, the rubric design, and the interpretation of results. Engineering owns the infrastructure. The investment scales with the product's complexity and the stakes involved.

Common Anti-Patterns (What NOT to Do)

Before we close this chapter, let me catalog the most common ways AI product teams get eval ownership wrong. Recognizing these anti-patterns will help you avoid them:

Anti-Pattern 1: "We'll Add Evals Later"
The team ships the AI feature first, plans to add evals "once things stabilize." Things never stabilize. New features get priority. Evals stay on the roadmap forever. Meanwhile, the AI degrades silently in production for months.

The fix: Build 10 test cases before you ship. Not after. Not in the next sprint. Before.
Anti-Pattern 2: "Our Users Are Our Eval"
The team relies on user feedback to identify quality problems. "If it's bad, users will tell us." This is like using your customers as your QA department. By the time they complain, the damage is done — they've already had a bad experience, already formed a negative impression, already considered switching.

The fix: Catch failures before users do. That's the entire point of evals. Users should encounter your best quality, not be the mechanism by which you discover your worst.
Anti-Pattern 3: "We Have Unit Tests, So We Have Evals"
The engineering team has unit tests that check if the API returns 200, if the response parses as valid JSON, if the model name is correct. These are infrastructure tests, not quality evals. They tell you the system is running. They don't tell you the system is producing good outputs. Passing all unit tests while shipping terrible AI outputs is the norm, not the exception.

The fix: Distinguish between infrastructure tests (engineering owns) and quality evals (PM owns). You need both. They're not substitutes for each other.
Anti-Pattern 4: "We Do Manual QA Before Every Release"
The PM or QA person manually tests 10-20 prompts before each deployment. This catches some issues but has three fatal flaws: (1) it's not repeatable — you test different things each time, (2) it doesn't catch regressions — you test the new feature but not everything else, and (3) it doesn't scale — as the product grows, 20 manual tests covers a smaller and smaller percentage of the surface area.

The fix: Manual review is valuable but insufficient. Turn your manual test prompts into automated test cases. Run ALL of them on every change, not just the ones you think are relevant.
Anti-Pattern 5: "Our Eval Suite Is Set and Forget"
A PM builds 50 test cases, integrates them into CI/CD, and moves on to other work. The eval suite runs on every deployment, scores stay green, and nobody touches the test cases for 6 months. Meanwhile, the product has launched 3 new features, the user base has shifted, and the failure modes have evolved. The eval suite is testing 6-month-old failure modes and missing the current ones.

The fix: The eval suite is a living document, not a one-time deliverable. Add 2-5 new test cases every week (from Monday output review). Remove test cases that are no longer relevant. Update rubric criteria as the product strategy evolves. An eval suite that isn't growing is an eval suite that's falling behind.

The Paradox of Eval Investment

There's a paradox that trips up many PMs when they first start owning evals: the teams that most need evals are the teams that feel they least have time for evals.

If your product has quality problems, you're spending time on firefighting: responding to user complaints, writing incident reports, rolling back bad changes, manually reviewing outputs. All of this is reactive work. It takes time. And when someone suggests "let's build an eval suite," the reaction is "we don't have time for that — we're too busy fighting fires."

But the fires exist because there are no evals. Without evals, you ship bad changes. Bad changes create fires. Fires consume your time. You don't have time for evals. So you ship more bad changes. More fires. It's a vicious cycle.

Evals break the cycle. A 6-hour investment in your first week (the Day 1-5 plan above) reduces the fire rate within the first sprint. By Week 4, the time you save on firefighting more than pays for the time you invested in the eval suite. By Month 3, you can't imagine working without them.

  The Eval Investment Paradox
  ===========================

  Without evals (vicious cycle):
  Ship blind → bad changes → fires → fight fires → no time
  → ship blind again → more fires → more fighting → ...

  With evals (virtuous cycle):
  Invest 6 hours → catch bad changes before shipping → fewer fires
  → time saved → invest in more test cases → even fewer fires
  → even more time saved → better product → ...

The initial investment breaks the vicious cycle and starts the virtuous one. The ROI is not future and hypothetical — it's visible within the first two weeks.
