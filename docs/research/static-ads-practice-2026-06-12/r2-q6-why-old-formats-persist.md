# r2-q6-why-old-formats-persist

Direct-response ad formats keep working because the **buyer psychology they exploit changes slowly**, while the *specific execution* gets stale and must be refreshed. Practitioners repeatedly describe this as a difference between a durable **format/angle** and temporary **creative fatigue**: the problem is usually not “PAS” or “testimonial” itself, but repeated exposure to the *same* creative, same hook, same frame, or same audience.[1][3][7][11][13]

- **Why the formats persist:** copywriting frameworks such as **PAS** are taught as durable because they map to a stable decision process: identify pain, intensify it, then present relief or proof.[2][4][6][10][14]
- **What actually dies:** sources on ad fatigue consistently say what burns out is the **creative**—the specific image, text, hook, or repeated exposure—not the underlying persuasion principle.[3][7][11][13][15]
- **How often a “proven format” stops working market-wide:** I could not find a credible study in the retrieved sources that quantifies a market-wide failure rate for formats like PAS, testimonial, before/after, us-vs-them, or advertorial. The available sources instead frame fatigue as *campaign- or audience-level saturation*, not a universal format death.[3][7][11][13][15]

The clearest practitioner explanation of durability is that **psychology is the constant and execution is the variable**. AdLibrary says to “identify the long-running ads in your category,” then classify the psychological angle in each long-runner and “build a frequency map” of what is winning in-market; it also says ads running **45, 60, 90 days** without pause are generating return, otherwise the spend would have stopped.[1] That is the core logic behind format durability: the market rewards recurring psychological patterns, but individual ads are replaced as they wear out.[1]

On PAS specifically, multiple sources describe the same structure in nearly identical terms. BlakSheep Creative says PAS is a “tried and true way” that begins with the **problem**, then **agitate[s]** those problems, then presents the **solution**.[2] Copywriting Matters says PAS works by identifying a pain point, making it “more emotional,” and then revealing the solution “that will be the balm your audience has been looking for.”[6] ReadstoLeads likewise describes PAS as a formula that “works well” because it moves from problem to agitation to solution.[4] These explanations imply the format endures because it mirrors how people notice pain and then evaluate relief.[2][4][6]

The same “constant psychology, changing execution” idea appears in broader ad-fatigue guidance. Amazon Ads says ad fatigue happens when audiences see the **same creative too often**, causing engagement to drop and costs to rise, and recommends **creative refresh** plus **ad rotation**.[7] Superside says one of the biggest causes is “old creative” and recommends frequent refreshing, even weekly or daily in some industries, while also warning not to keep using the **same format across platforms**.[3] Motion similarly says creative fatigue is when engagement drops despite stable frequency and unchanged targeting, and recommends rotating specific elements, especially the **hook**.[11] Viant adds that “people aren’t anti-ad. They’re anti-irrelevant, anti-intrusive, and anti-boring,” and says “If your creative hasn’t changed in three campaigns, your audience noticed—and left.”[13]

That distinction explains why **testimonial**, **before/after**, **us-vs-them**, and **advertorial** can last for decades. Those are not just aesthetics; they are reusable persuasion containers for social proof, contrast, identity, and narrative immersion. ReadstoLeads explicitly lists **testimonials** and case-study-type proof as elements that can serve as “proof” inside a persuasive sequence.[4] The YouTube practitioner commentary in the retrieved results says copywriting icons introduced “testimonials,” “speaking to a consumer’s deepest desires,” and “painting a picture of what life could look like once you use this product,” and that these methods “are still used today.”[12] AdLibrary also points to cognitive biases such as **social proof**, **scarcity**, **loss aversion**, and **anchoring** as the actual mechanisms that need to be mapped to executions.[1]

What causes an ad format to die, according to the retrieved sources, is usually one of three things:

- **Exposure saturation:** the same creative seen too often, leading to fatigue and declining engagement.[7][11][15]
- **Relevance failure:** the ad no longer matches the audience’s mindset, environment, or moment.[13]
- **Stagnant execution:** the hook, angle, or visual pattern has not been refreshed, even if the format name stayed the same.[1][3][11]

That is also why experts warn against confusing format with execution. AdLibrary says “Psychology in advertisement is only useful when mapped to specific executions,” and that there is “No single principle” that wins universally.[1] Motion says to prioritize updates to the **hook** because it sits at the top of the “creative hierarchy.”[11] In other words, a format usually does not die because humans stop responding to it; it dies when the market has seen that particular expression too many times, or when the offer/audience/context no longer fits the frame.[1][3][7][11][13]
