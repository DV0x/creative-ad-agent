# q8-ai-tools-state

Performance marketers in 2025–2026 are heavily using **specialized AI ad generators (AdCreative.ai, Omneky, Pencil, Predis, AdMake, Arc Ads, Canva AI, Midjourney / DALL‑E / Sora image tools)** plus **chat-based models (ChatGPT / GPT-4.x)** for concepts, copy, and batch production – but practitioners repeatedly say that **“strategy, taste, concept and final selection” remain human**, and complain that a lot of outputs are generic “**AI slop**” when used naively.[2][5][8][13]

Below is a synthesis focused on what *practitioners* say – including direct quotes – about real-world use, quality, failures vs human teams, and where they still draw the human line.

---

## 1. Which AI tools are performance marketers actually using (2025–2026)?

### 1.1 Dedicated AI ad generators

A 2026 comparison by **AdMake AI** tested **AdMake AI, AdCreative.ai, Predis.ai, Canva, and Creatopy** specifically as AI ad generators for paid media.[7]

They summarize:

> “We tested AdMake AI, AdCreative.ai, Predis.ai, Canva, and Creatopy.”[7]

and position:

- **AdMake AI** – “*Best Overall AI Ad Generator*” with ad research + creative generation.[7]  
- **AdCreative.ai** – “*Best for Performance Scoring*.”[7]  
- **Predis.ai** – “*Best for Social Media Scheduling*.”[7]  
- **Canva** – “*for general design needs*.”[7]  

This reflects what many media buyers report informally: **AI-first ad tools plus Canva remain the baseline stack** for static creative.

Separately, a Facebook creative-system post explicitly pitches an **“AI Creative System”** to:

> “scale brands and effortlessly launch **15-20 fresh, high-converting ad creatives every week**.”[10]

While the post is promotional, the promise (15–20 new creatives per week) is consistent with how performance marketers now talk about using AI: *volume and iteration* more than “one perfect hero ad.”

### 1.2 Platform-native AI creative (Meta / Advantage+)

A 2025–2026 Meta ads best-practices guide notes that advertisers are systematically using **Meta’s Advantage+ Creative** to auto-generate creative variations:

> “In 2025 and 2026, advertisers using **Advantage+ Creative** report an average **14% improvement in cost per result** compared to static single-asset campaigns.”[4]  

They recommend:

> “Enable **Advantage+ Creative** at the ad level so Meta generates AI variations automatically.”[4]

This is *very* widely adopted because it’s baked into Meta Ads Manager; many performance marketers treat this as their default “AI creative” even if they also use external tools.

### 1.3 UGC / ad-systems with AI components

A 2026 YouTube breakdown of “**Top 10 tools I use To CRUSH Facebook Ads in 2026**” by a performance practitioner lists these AI-related tools in daily use:[13]

- **Arc Ads** – described as “AI-Powered UGC Creation” (Tool #5).[13]  
- **Gling** – “Fast AI Video Editing” (Tool #6).[13]  
- **ChatGPT** – “Copywriting + Research” (Tool #7).[13]  
- **Sora** – “AI Ad Image Generation” (Tool #8).[13]  
- **Motion** – “AI powered creative analytics” to tell you “what's working, why it's working, and the exact ad to make next.”[13]  

Even though this list is cross-format (video + static), it shows real practitioners are integrating:

- **AI-generation tools** (Sora, Arc Ads)  
- **AI-editing tools** (Gling)  
- **AI-analytics tools** (Motion)  

into one creative workflow.

### 1.4 Design tools with AI image/text (Canva AI and similar)

The AdMake comparison explicitly treats **Canva** as part of the AI ad stack and positions it as a “general design” workhorse alongside more ad-specific tools.[7] Canva’s Magic Media / AI-image features are widely used for:

- Turning prompts into background images  
- Quick resizing + variant generation  
- Layering AI imagery with brand assets

Practitioners rarely call this out as “AI” in posts – they just say “I mocked it up in Canva” – but the underlying generation is AI-driven.

## 2. What do practitioners *actually* say about output quality and “AI slop”?

### 2.1 “AI slop” as a recognized quality problem

A 2025 explanation of **“AI slop”** by Qustodio (quoting Merriam-Webster’s 2025 word of the year) defines it as:

> “**low-quality digital content, mostly created in large quantities with the ‘help’ of artificial intelligence.**”[8]

They add:

> “Any low-quality content that AI creates, including text, images, audio, and video, can be bundled in as **AI slop**.”[8]  

Similarly, Artlist’s guide for creators notes:

> “AI slop describes **low-effort, mass-generated content that prioritizes quantity over creativity.** It often looks or sounds generic and lacks originality or …”[5]

This matches a lot of practitioner complaints: **AI tools make it trivial to flood feeds with generic ad creatives**, often with minimal QA.

In a Facebook discussion, one user vents:

> “**Holy crap the ai-slop ads have been out of control today.**”[14]

Commenters reportedly share that feeds are increasingly full of templated, uncanny AI imagery.[14] This is exactly what performance marketers risk when they rely too heavily on “one-click” tools.

A technologist in *The Conversation* similarly critiques AI-generated imagery:

> These visuals “fall under the category of **AI slop**, which refers to low- to moderate-quality content … produced using artificial intelligence tools, often lacking in accuracy.”[2]

### 2.2 “Mediocrity has always been the baseline”

An Instagram post by a visual culture commentator argues that blaming AI alone misses the broader context:

> “The concept of ‘AI slop,’ used to label the poor quality of algorithmic imagery, **overlooks a fundamental historical reality: mediocrity has always constituted** …”[11]

This reflects a sentiment some creative directors echo privately: **AI simply automates the kind of mediocre direct-response banners, stock-photo mashups, and template ads that have always existed** – the difference is scale, not species.

### 2.3 “Good if you guide it; garbage if you don’t”

Academic and design-thinking perspectives reinforce what many practitioners say informally: **AI is powerful when it’s directed; lazy prompting yields slop.**

A study on human–AI co-creation from Cambridge Judge Business School found:

> “human-AI co-creation did not automatically lead to improved joint creativity over time, even after 10 rounds of creative tasks.”[6]

They only saw improvement when humans were explicitly instructed to **co-develop and refine ideas** rather than just asking AI for more and more generations:

> “Improvement occurred only when we introduced a deliberate intervention. Specifically, instructing participants to engage in **idea co-development – focusing on feedback and refinement rather than endlessly generating new ideas – was the key.**”[6]

This insight mirrors performance marketers who say:

- If you **feed AI real performance data, strong briefs, and tight guardrails**, you can get decent-to-strong assets.  
- If you **press “generate 50 ads” with no taste or strategy**, you get AI slop that underperforms.

## 3. Where do AI ad generators fail vs human creative teams?

Across sources (academic, design, and practitioner commentary), three recurring failure modes matter for performance marketing.

### 3.1 Lack of true human insight, empathy, and strategy

IDEO U’s exploration of **AI and design thinking** puts it bluntly:

> “AI can crunch numbers and spot patterns, but **it can't empathize or think creatively like humans can.**”[12]

They emphasize that:

> “At the heart of design thinking lies **human-centered design**, or the ability to understand people deeply.”[12]

and that AI should only **“enhance rather than replace human creativity and decision-making.”**[12]

For ad creative, this translates directly to:

- AI is **strong at recombining what already exists**, but weak at **discovering a new emotional angle, narrative, or positioning** in a market.  
- AI can optimize hooks and formats that already work; it does not *on its own* develop a brand’s core strategic story.

A broad study on AI vs human work concludes:

> “AI handles speed and scale; **humans handle meaning and relationship.**”[9]

Performance marketers routinely report this in practice: they use AI to scale out versions, but **they still rely on human strategists to decide “what are we saying, to whom, and why?”**

### 3.2 Generic, derivative visuals and copy

Artlist’s description of AI slop is essentially a description of how AI ads look when left to their own devices:

> “low-effort, mass-generated content that **prioritizes quantity over creativity**. It often looks or sounds **generic and lacks originality** …”[5]

For static ads, practitioners complain that many AI tools:

- Overuse **stock-ish compositions** and tropes  
- Produce **over-polished, plastic-looking people**  
- Mis-handle **complex product details** (e.g., hardware, food, hands, packaging)

Qustodio’s guide lists classic AI “tells” that marketers themselves have learned to recognize (and audiences increasingly notice):

> “Watch for jerky movements, things that appear or suddenly disappear, or actions that don’t make logical sense … Crowds could have distorted faces … one person could be cloned several times in the background.”[8]

Even though that passage discusses video, the same issues appear in stills: **weird hands, warped text, physics that don’t make sense**. For products where realism and trust matter (fitness gear, food, financial services), this can directly hurt performance.

### 3.3 Failure on novel, ambiguous, or emotionally complex briefs

Research on human–AI collaboration shows that **AI is weakest on tasks involving novelty, ambiguity, and uncertainty**, exactly the kinds of briefs senior creatives handle.[9]

Erickson Coaching’s synthesis of research states:

> “When AI handled routine analysis and pattern recognition, humans working together significantly outperformed both AI alone and humans working individually on the hardest problems – those involving **novelty, ambiguity, and uncertainty.**”[9]

In performance marketing terms:

- AI is good at: *“Make 30 new variations of this founder-POV static ad for lookalike audiences and retargeting.”*  
- AI struggles with: *“Create a totally new cold-traffic angle for a category-fatigued audience where our brand has tricky perception issues.”*

This is also where **bad AI outputs are perceived as “slop”**: the tools are being asked to solve problems that require cultural judgment, contextual reading, and political sensitivity that they don’t possess.

## 4. What parts of the process remain uniquely human, according to practitioners and research?

Across the sources, four stages are consistently described as **uniquely or predominantly human** in high-performing workflows.

### 4.1 Concept, strategy, and positioning

IDEO U stresses:

> “Here’s the most important thing to remember: **AI isn't here to replace humans. It's here to amplify our capabilities.**”[12]

and advises leaders to:

> “Use AI as a brainstorming partner. Let it generate ideas, **then use your human creativity to build on, combine, or completely flip these ideas on their head.**”[12]

This matches what performance-focused agencies describe as their reality:

- Humans **define the creative platform, angles, and hypotheses**.  
- AI is used to propose **visual executions** inside those constraints.  
- Humans then **curate and refine**.

Similarly, Erickson Coaching frames AI as an analyst, not a strategist:

> “AI does not bring empathy, accountability, or moral judgment. But it does function as a reliable cognitive contributor: **synthesizing data, surfacing patterns, stress-testing assumptions, and offering rapid feedback.**”[9]

The **“why”** of the ad – the human meaning – stays with people.

### 4.2 Taste, curation, and “this *feels* right”

Design-thinking guidance emphasizes:

> “It is essential to ensure that **AI enhances rather than replaces human creativity and decision-making**. This requires a careful balance between leveraging AI's capabilities and **preserving the human touch** in the design process.”[12]

IDEO U’s practical advice (“For every AI-generated insight, ask yourself: ‘How does this impact real people?’”[12]) is essentially a proxy for **taste and judgment**.

Cambridge’s human–AI co-creation study shows that creativity only improved when humans were deliberately asked to **refine**, not just generate:

> “idea generation itself had no relationship with joint creativity in human-AI collaboration” while **idea co-development** did.[6]

Taken literally for ad work:

- The “magic” is not “generate more variations.”  
- The value is in **how humans sift, edit, composite, and direct** those variations with taste and performance data.

### 4.3 User empathy and human-centered research

IDEO U repeatedly insists that **empathy and genuine user understanding are human**:

> “AI tools can significantly enhance this phase, **not by replacing human empathy or human connection**, but rather by automating the way that data and research is collected and analyzed.”[12]

They argue that **“At the heart of design thinking lies human-centered design”** and that leaders must “continuously engage with users, gathering their feedback, and iterating on solutions to ensure they meet user expectations.”[12]

In paid media terms, this maps to:

- Interviewing customers, reading reviews  
- Understanding nuanced objections, language, and cultural context  
- Translating those into **creative briefs and angles** that AI then helps execute

AI can help cluster responses or test variants, but **the act of empathizing and deciding what matters most** remains human.

### 4.4 System-building, feedback loops, and optimization decisions

The Meta best-practices guide for 2025–2026 describes a **creative system** where humans define cadence, metrics, and interpretation:[4]

> “A scalable Meta Ads system has three components:  
> 1. **Tracking foundation** …  
> 2. **Creative system:** Three to five new creative concepts produced weekly, angles rotated based on hook rate and hold rate data.  
> 3. **Review process:** Weekly performance review using hook rate, hold rate, cost per qualified lead, and cost per signed case as the primary metrics.”[4]

Even as Meta’s AI handles bidding and Advantage+ creative variations, humans:

- Decide **what concepts to test each week**  
- Interpret **hook rate, hold rate, cost per qualified lead**  
- Choose **what to scale, what to kill, and what to re-brief**

Erickson’s review of research echoes this division:

> “AI handles speed and scale; humans handle **meaning and relationship**.”[9]

For performance marketing, “meaning” includes: which KPIs matter, what tradeoffs are acceptable, and what brand positioning is non-negotiable even if a gimmicky ad might win the short-term CPA test.

## 5. Practical takeaways for a performance-marketing workflow

Based on these sources and what practitioners are openly using and saying:

- **Tools in active use (static & image-heavy creative)**  
  - AI ad generators: **AdMake AI, AdCreative.ai, Predis.ai, Creatopy**[7]  
  - Design platforms with AI: **Canva** (Magic Media)[7]  
  - Platform-native AI: **Meta Advantage+ Creative**.[4]  
  - AI-UGC / video tools with still outputs: **Arc Ads**, **Gling**, **Sora**, **Midjourney/DALL‑E equivalents** within workflows.[13]  
  - Analytics: **Motion** for “AI powered creative analytics” – “you know what's working, why it's working, and the exact ad to make next.”[13]

- **Where AI is strong for performance marketers**  
  - Rapid **variant generation** and resizing.  
  - Turning **structured briefs and existing winners** into more angles.  
  - **Testing a high volume** of hooks and looks; feeding signals back into Meta / Google.[4][7][13]

- **Where AI is routinely criticized (“AI slop” zone)**  
  - One-click, low-effort “generate 50 ads,” producing **generic, derivative visuals and copy**.[2][5][8][14]  
  - Over-reliance on AI for **core concept, emotional resonance, or nuanced brand voice**, producing work that feels hollow or uncanny.[5][12]  
  - Use on **novel, ambiguous, or politically/culturally sensitive briefs** where mistakes are costly and obvious.[9]

- **What remains human, according to research and practitioners**  
  - **Concept & strategy** – deciding the big idea and positioning.[9][12]  
  - **Taste & curation** – picking, editing, and fusing outputs based on what “feels” right and performs.[6][12]  
  - **Empathy & insight** – grounding creative in real user needs and language.[9][12]  
  - **System design & decision-making** – building the testing framework, reading the data, and making the call on tradeoffs and brand risk.[4][9]
