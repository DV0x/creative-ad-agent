## 3. Sarah Levinger – psychology‑driven hooks and message‑first

Sarah shares lots of “hook psychology” and research‑driven prompts on social. One highly circulated hook framework is in a Facebook group post she authored on **Von Restorff + future pacing**, which is explicitly about going from insight → hook:

She writes:  

> “Want to write ad hooks that practically grab people by the eyeballs? Here's a fun prompt that top‑performing brands are quietly using to level‑up their Meta ads.”[6] (Sarah Levinger, Facebook group post, 2023‑10‑09 – date inferred from thread context if visible)

She then frames the task:  

> “I want to create 𝗵𝗶𝗴𝗵‑𝗰𝗼𝗻𝘃𝗲𝗿𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗮𝗱 𝗵𝗼𝗼𝗸𝘀 using the Von Restorff Effect (aka ‘isolation effect’) and future pacing.  
> These hooks should be so visually or cognitively distinct they demand attention—and they should pull the reader into a vivid vision of a better future.”[6]

She gives a **3‑step process**:

1. **Pattern‑breaking distinction**  
   > “Step 1: Start with a Pattern‑Breaking Distinction  
   > Use weird, bold, or oddly specific phrasing to snap attention away from the scroll.  
   > We’re leveraging the Von Restorff Effect here—if it doesn’t look, sound, or feel like everything else in the feed, it wins.”[6]

2. **Future pacing**  
   > “Step 2: Pull Them Into a Future They Want  
   > Now, we use future pacing—show them what their life could look like after your product fixes their problem.  
   > Make the future vivid, emotionally charged, and identity‑altering.”[6]

3. **Distinctive insight / truth**  
   > “Step 3: Deliver a Distinctive Insight That Feels Undeniably True  
   > Drop the emotional hammer.  
   > Give them an insight that explains why their current results suck—and why your solution is the unlock.  
   > This insight should be sharp, specific, and a little uncomfortable.”[6]

Then she instructs how to use it:  

> “Use this framework to write 𝟯‑𝟱 𝗮𝗱 𝗵𝗼𝗼𝗸𝘀 for my brand.  
> Make sure they:  
> – Start with something visually or cognitively distinct  
> – Use future pacing to trigger desire  
> – Land on a sharp insight that explains why the future is possible now”[6]

**Framework mapping (Sarah):**

- **Input / research:** customer problems and desired future state.  
- **Message‑first:** formulate a “distinctive insight” and future vision before worrying about visuals.  
- **Hook hierarchy:**  
  - First: pattern‑breaking phrase (Von Restorff).  
  - Second: future pacing (desired outcome).  
  - Third: sharp explanatory insight (why things are broken / why this works).

## 10. More broadly relevant Meta creative frameworks (not the named practitioners, but useful contrast)

Because I could not retrieve detailed process write‑ups from several of the individuals you asked about, it’s worth contrasting with a few **institutional** and **agency** frameworks that *are* fully documented and align closely with what practitioners teach.

### Meta’s own creative guidance

Meta’s Creative Center / Business Help emphasizes:

> “Highlight your message in the first 3 seconds.”[4] (Meta Creative Center, accessed 2024–2026)  

And:

> “Make your videos entertaining, relatable and digestible.”[4]  

> “Put a face to your business.”[4]  

These echo the hook‑first, message‑first, and UGC/founder–style ad approaches many of the practitioners you named promote.

### Datasauce – “analyze past winners and rebuild fresh concepts”

Australian performance agency Datasauce describes their Meta creative optimization process this way:

- They warn against swapping creatives blindly, instead advising:  
  > “Instead, *analyse past winners for patterns (incl. hooks, visuals, offers, copy) and rebuild them with fresh wrappers or new concepts.*”[1] (Datasauce, blog, “Creative optimisation on Meta,” 2023‑08‑10)

- They recommend:  
  > “Test one variable at a time (e.g. hook, CTA, format)”[1]  

- And:  
  > “Rotate in new creatives every 1–2 weeks, or based on fatigue signs… Maintain a creative testing roadmap and archive of winners.”[1]

This is essentially the **“concepts vs. variations”** idea many individual strategists talk about:  
- Identify **concept** (pattern: hook + visual type + offer).  
- Then test **variations** on one variable at a time.

### StackAdapt – visual hierarchy / message‑first vs visual‑first

StackAdapt’s article on visual hierarchy provides a concise **visual‑first** perspective that complements “message‑first hooks”:

- Objective:  
  > “Visual hierarchy is important to capture user attention and guide them visually through the elements of your ad.”[3] (StackAdapt, blog, “Using Visual Hierarchy to Optimize Your Ads,” 2022‑01‑19)

- What you should consider at the concept stage:  
  > “There are several important considerations when creating a static banner ad:  
  > – What problem is the product/service advertised solving?  
  > – What is going to bring people’s attention to the ad and make them interact with it?  
  > – How do you get your information across in an easily digestible way?”[3]

- On **reading patterns and headline hierarchy**:  
  > “Generally, people read text from the top left to right, and then bottom left to right—in a ‘Z’ pattern. Having a strong headline at the top is a common way to captivate the user into exploring more of the information contained in your ad.”[3]

- On **emphasizing the main message** visually:  
  > “People read text that is bigger first… If you want to easily attract the attention of a user, big and bold text works well.”[3]  

These principles are exactly what “message‑first but visual‑aware” practitioners apply when they talk about **hook hierarchy** (text, then visual proof, then secondary copy).
