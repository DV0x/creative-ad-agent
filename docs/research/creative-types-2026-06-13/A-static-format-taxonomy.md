## PART 2 — VISUAL ANATOMY PER FORMAT

Each entry describes: (a) layout structure and zones, (b) photography vs. graphic vs. screenshot, (c) role of typography, (d) where the craft lives.

---

### 1. Us-vs-Them Comparison

**Layout:** Two-column split. Left = the alternative (generic / competitor / old way). Right = your product (winner column). 3–6 comparison rows running horizontally: ingredient count, price, certifications, taste ratings, delivery time — whatever the buyer cares about. Bottom: brand + CTA. Optional header: "Why we're different" or just the category problem.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Mostly graphic/designed. Clean product shots of both options at top of each column. The "winner" column often gets a highlight color or checkmark trail; loser column gets muted tones or X marks. Real product photography (pack shot) in each column header.

**Typography role:** Row labels in mid-weight sans-serif; values in bold numerals or short words. The winner column's values in brand color. Header is the biggest text on the canvas — 1 line, punchy claim.

**Where craft lives:** The choice of comparison criteria. Generic criteria (price, reviews) = commodity. Category-specific criteria that the buyer already tracks (e.g., "no proprietary blends," "third-party tested," "ships same day") = the conversion lever. The layout is commodity; the criteria selection is where the creative team earns its fee.

**Texture signal:** Low-to-medium native feel. Clearly a designed ad, but the tabular structure feels utilitarian/informational rather than marketing-glossy. Competitor brands are rarely named by name (policy risk); hinted via "other brands" or category-generic packaging.

---

### 2. Testimonial / Review Screenshot

**Layout:** Three focal zones, equal weight: (1) Customer face/photo (top or left), (2) verbatim quote — 1–3 sentences max, most specific sentence highlighted in bold or brand color, (3) star rating rendered as emoji or graphic stars + name/handle. Product visual or brand mark in corner. CTA below or outside the image.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Hybrid. Real customer photo is essential — headshot or lifestyle selfie, never stock. Background: clean flat color or blurred lifestyle. The quote itself is typeset, not handwritten.

**Typography role:** Quote is the hero. 24–30pt, clean serif or humanist sans. Attribution (first name, city or job title) in smaller supporting type. Star rating is visual, not a copy claim. "Real Customer Review" badge optional.

**Where craft lives:** Quote selection. "Love it!" does nothing. "I stopped my migraine meds after 3 weeks" or "Cut our weekly creative production from 6 hours to 45 minutes" — specificity is what makes the format convert. The harder job is sourcing the quote, not designing the card.

**Texture signal:** Mid-range native feel. More polished than a screenshot format, less polished than a designed poster. Using an actual customer photo and verbatim phrasing (typos included) pushes it toward authentic. Airbrushed stock-style headshots push it toward generic.

---

### 3. Listicle / "Top Reasons" / Numbered Benefits

**Layout:** Vertical stack. Header: "[Number] reasons [brand] [outcome]" in 1–2 lines. Then 3–7 rows, each: icon (left) + short benefit statement (right). Bottom: product visual or CTA badge.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Predominantly graphic. Flat icons or emoji. Product photography appears as small inset in top corner or bottom. Background is clean flat color (brand palette) or white.

**Typography role:** Central. The list IS the content. Numbers/bullets draw the eye down the page. Each line item is 5–8 words — scannable, never prose. Top item = strongest benefit (first or last position is most recalled).

**Where craft lives:** Benefit wording. Generic benefit copy ("High quality," "Great taste") makes the format useless. Specific, unexpected benefits ("No corn syrup," "Ships in biodegradable packaging," "Your HR team stops emailing you") is what makes someone stop. The icon set should be consistent and minimal; mismatched clip-art icons destroy credibility.

**Texture signal:** Clearly designed. Sits toward the polished/graphic end of the spectrum. Does not read as native content. Works because it's informational/useful, not because it's organic-feeling.

---

### 4. Problem-Solution / PAS (Problem-Agitate-Solution)

**Layout:** Three-panel flow within a single frame. (1) Problem statement in headline position — 1 short sentence, pain-first language. (2) Agitation: brief expansion, 1–2 lines showing consequence. (3) Product visual + solution headline + metric ("In 7 days" / "Without the crash"). CTA at base.

**Alternative layout:** Pure text in upper half (problem in large type) + product image in lower half with solution copy overlaid.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Photography for the solution panel (product shot or lifestyle image of the resolved state). Problem panel is often pure typography on flat color or abstract texture.

**Typography role:** Problem headline is the hook — needs to be the biggest, sharpest text. If the reader doesn't recognize their problem in 1.5 seconds, the format fails entirely.

**Where craft lives:** Problem specificity. "Tired of being tired?" is a dead hook (used by every energy brand). "Still crashing at 2pm even though you slept 8 hours?" is where attention starts. The problem sentence does 80% of the work.

**Texture signal:** Variable. Text-dominant problem panel reads fairly native. Product image in solution panel makes it clearly promotional. Overall: mid-spectrum.

---

### 5. Offer / Promo / Discount Card

**Layout:** Anchor element: the discount (% or $) in oversized bold type, center canvas. Secondary: what's on offer (product name or category). Tertiary: deadline ("Ends Sunday") or scarcity ("Only 47 left"). Product visual occupies 30–40% of frame, often bottom or side. CTA button implied (clicks to offer page).

**Photography vs. graphic:** Graphic-dominant. Bold background color (brand color or high-contrast). Product as secondary visual element.

**Typography role:** The number IS the creative. "40% OFF" set at 80% canvas-width in black on white or white on brand color. Supporting copy minimal — 1 line deadline, 1 line offer scope.

**Where craft lives:** In the offer itself (not in the design). A 10% discount formatted beautifully loses to a 40% discount formatted badly. But given competitive parity on offer, the framing matters: "Save $30" vs "Get $30 back" vs "Only pay $39 today" hit differently even at identical economics.

**Texture signal:** Explicitly promotional, low-native. Nobody mistakes a discount card for organic content. That is fine — warm audiences know what they want and just need the deal confirmed. Using this format cold is a mismatch.

---

### 6. Founder Story / Founder POV

**Layout (static version):** Full-bleed founder photo — real face, real space (kitchen, office, factory floor), not a studio. Overlaid headline: 1–2 sentences in the founder's own voice. Supporting copy if any: 1 sentence origin story. Brand logo secondary. CTA.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Photography is the whole format. The photo must feel real — not corporate headshot, not studio lighting. Ideally shot on the founder's phone or with natural light. The face is the hook.

**Typography role:** Minimal. The photo does the work. Headline is founder's POV, written in first person: "I built this because I was sick of supplements full of fillers." No marketing language.

**Where craft lives:** The photo (authenticity of setting + expression) and the POV sentence. If the sentence sounds like a brand campaign, the format collapses. It must sound like a real person's specific grievance.

**Texture signal:** High native feel. Photographic, personal, imperfect. Sits close to the organic-content end of the spectrum. The less designed it looks, the better it works.

---

### 7. Product Demo / Feature Callout / "How It Works"

**Layout:** Product image (hero) + labeled callout lines radiating from specific features, OR a step-flow diagram (Step 1 → Step 2 → Step 3 → Result). Callout variant: arrows or lines from specific product zones to short benefit labels in the margin.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Product photography is essential. The callout lines and labels are graphic overlays. The product must be real, high-quality, well-lit. The diagram version uses photography + flat graphic arrows + typeset step labels.

**Typography role:** Feature labels must be ultra-short (2–5 words each). "Marine collagen peptides," "No artificial sweeteners," "Dissolves in 30 sec." Each label is a mini-claim.

**Where craft lives:** Which features to call out and how to phrase them. Calling out generic features ("Great taste," "Easy to use") = useless. Calling out unexpected or differentiating mechanisms ("300mg active NMN per serve," "No proprietary blend — see every dose") = conversion lever. The diagram version lives or dies on whether the step-flow reveals a genuine mechanism advantage.

**Texture signal:** Mid-spectrum. More designed than a lifestyle photo, less corporate than a polished poster. The arrow/callout aesthetic has an informational/scientific register that can feel authoritative without feeling slick.

---

### 8. Before / After

*(Note: BANNED on Meta for health/supplements categories. Compliant for: hair, home organization, SaaS dashboards, skin without medical health claims, fitness non-injury.)*

**Layout:** Two halves, vertical or horizontal split. Clear delineation line or label ("Before" / "After" — or avoid those exact words with "Then" / "Now" for partial policy compliance). Real user transformation photo or product result photo. Caption: specific metric overlaid ("Week 1" → "Week 8").

**Photography vs. graphic:** Photography entirely. Real users, real spaces. Studio photography reads as staged and loses credibility.

**Typography role:** Minimal. Labels + metric. The visual contrast is the message.

**Where craft lives:** Image quality and believability. Dramatic but believable transformation. Overly dramatic (too-good-to-be-true) drops conversion by triggering skepticism.

**Texture signal:** Mid-native. Photographic = organic feel. But the split-screen structure is clearly designed. Feels closer to a real-life documentary than a poster.

---

### 9. Notes App / iMessage / Native Screenshot

**Layout:** Mimics the iPhone Notes app interface or iMessage thread. Black or dark heading bar. White body. Content: bullet points with checkmarks or emojis. Text written as if the user is making personal notes or sending a message to a friend. Often includes: personal opener, 3–5 bullet points as reasons/benefits, closing call-to-action disguised as a note to self ("order more before it runs out").

**Photography vs. graphic:** This IS a screenshot (real or recreated). No product photography. No brand design. Just the OS interface as the format.

**Typography role:** Uses system font (San Francisco for iOS). The typography itself signals authenticity — it's the same font people see in their own phone all day. Any other font breaks the illusion.

**Where craft lives:** The copy. The voice must be genuinely personal — casual, specific, first-person. "Morning Brew condenses to 25 words" is the model: tight, real-feeling, ends on action. Over-copywritten notes ads are easy to spot and the format fails.

**Texture signal:** Extremely high native feel. The interface is the authenticity signal. Viewers' brains process the OS chrome subconsciously as "personal device" before the ad-filter reflex fires.

---

### 10. Meme / Tweet / Internet-Native Format

**Layout:** Mimics a real internet format — tweet screenshot, Reddit comment, reaction image with caption, "starter pack" grid (2x2 with labels), or viral template. Brand product appears as one element, not the hero. Humor or cultural recognition is the hook.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Depends on sub-format. Tweet: screenshot aesthetic (graphic). Starter pack: mixed grid (photography + text labels + meme images). Reaction: photographic.

**Typography role:** Platform-native fonts and formatting. Twitter/X uses Chirp. Reddit uses system fonts. Any font substitution reads as fake. Meme captions use Impact or Arial Black in traditional formats.

**Where craft lives:** The idea match — does the cultural format earn the brand a laugh, or does it feel forced? The worst meme ads are clearly-a-brand-trying-to-meme. The best ones work as standalone content that happens to have a product in it.

**Texture signal:** Very high native feel. The format IS organic content. But it's also the easiest to do badly. Authenticity requirement is highest here.

---

### 11. Infographic / Educational

**Layout:** Vertical information architecture. Header: bold question or claim. Body: 3–6 sections with icons, data points, short explanations. Footer: brand attribution + CTA. Data visualization (simple bar, pie, or comparison chart) optional.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Graphic dominant. Product photography as secondary element. Color palette follows brand.

**Typography role:** Hierarchy is critical. Three type sizes minimum: header, section titles, body data. Consistent across sections for scannability.

**Where craft lives:** Information selection and credibility of sources. An infographic that cites real studies reads differently from one that cites the brand's own marketing. The format implicitly promises objectivity, so biased data destroys trust.

**Texture signal:** Clearly designed, low-native. Sits at the polished end. Works not through authenticity but through genuine usefulness — the ad earns attention by teaching something.

---

### 12. Single Stat or Quote Card

**Layout:** Near-empty canvas. One number or one sentence in enormous type. Brand mark in corner. CTA minimal or absent (curiosity drives the click). Example: "73% of our customers canceled their gym membership" (SaaS fitness app).

**Photography vs. graphic:** Graphic. Clean background. Sometimes a subtle product image or texture behind the stat.

**Typography role:** The entire creative. The number or sentence must be set so large that it reads at thumbnail size. Everything else is negative space.

**Where craft lives:** Stat selection. The stat must be genuinely surprising, specific, and relevant to the buyer's life. "Over 10,000 customers" = ignored. "The average customer loses 14 hours/month to this problem" = stop-worthy.

**Texture signal:** Mid-spectrum. Minimalism reads as confident/premium. But it's clearly designed. The less copy on canvas, the more the stat itself must earn the attention.

---

### 13. What's Inside / Ingredients / Clean Label

**Layout:** Product pack shot (front label visible) + ingredient callout list alongside. OR: two-column table: "What's IN it" vs "What's NOT in it." OR: Zoomed-in pack label with handwritten-style annotations circling the clean ingredients.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Mixed. Real product photography is essential (the physical label is the proof). Graphic overlays for callout labels or comparison columns.

**Typography role:** Label typography must match the product pack (authenticity). Callout labels in secondary supporting font. "What's NOT in it" list often uses strikethrough or crossed-out red type for visual impact.

**Where craft lives:** Ingredient selection and naming. "Magnesium" = generic. "Magnesium bisglycinate (the form your body actually absorbs)" = mechanism claim that educates and differentiates. The format only works if the ingredient story is genuinely differentiating from category norms.

**Texture signal:** Mid-to-high native feel. Real product photography anchors credibility. Label closeup reads as research/investigation rather than promotion.

---

### 14. Bundle / Value Stack

**Layout:** Multiple product images arranged on canvas (flat-lay, stack, or floating arrangement). Large header: the deal ("Everything you need to [outcome]"). Price comparison: "$180 value / $79 today." List of included items with individual values. Guarantee or risk-reversal badge. CTA.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Product photography (individual pack shots or lifestyle) + graphic price/value labels.

**Typography role:** The price strike-through ("$180 → $79") is typographic. Large, high-contrast. Everything else is supporting hierarchy.

**Where craft lives:** The perceived value of items in the bundle. If the bundle contains "bonus" items buyers don't want, the stack feels padded. If every item in the bundle is genuinely desirable, the stack math creates real value pressure.

**Texture signal:** Clearly promotional, moderate-low native. Explicitly a deal ad. Best for warm audiences who are sold on the category and just need a reason to act now.

---

### 15. Lifestyle / Product-in-Use

**Layout:** Full-bleed photography — real person in real context using the product. Product is present but not the hero; the person/moment is. Copy minimal: headline overlay (1 line, benefit or aspiration) in corner, brand mark secondary.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Photography is the entire format. The quality and authenticity of the photo is the craft. Studio-lit lifestyle photos lose to genuinely candid or realistically art-directed photography.

**Typography role:** Minimal overlay. The photo must work without copy. If you need the headline to explain what's happening, the photo has failed.

**Where craft lives:** Model casting, setting, and activity specificity. Generic "woman smiling on beach" = stock photo energy. "35-year-old woman in a messy kitchen making coffee with the product in frame" = real and relatable. The more specific the scene, the stronger the identification.

**Texture signal:** High native feel when photography is authentic and specific. Drops sharply when photography is stock-style (perfect lighting, too-attractive models, artificially clean environments).

---

### 16. Hero Product Shot

**Layout:** Product isolated on minimal background — flat color, gradient, or dark void. No model, no copy, sometimes no headline. Product is the entire creative. Lighting is the craft.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Photography + expert lighting + post-processing. This is the format most damaged by AI generation (AI product shots have characteristic lighting/shadow artifacts that trained eyes identify immediately in 2026).

**Typography role:** None required. If copy is added, it's 1–3 words ("Pure. Simple. Effective.") in restrained font at canvas bottom.

**Where craft lives:** Lighting, texture rendering, shadow, material representation. A hero product shot that doesn't make the packaging look beautiful/real is useless. Craft IS the format.

**Texture signal:** Clearly premium/designed. Does not read as native. Works for warm audiences who already want the product — the shot confirms quality. Cold traffic generally needs more context before a product shot alone converts.

---

### 17. Advertorial / Editorial Card

**Layout:** Mimics a magazine article or news card. Header: editorial-style headline ("The supplement that dermatologists actually recommend"). Subhead: one sentence expanding the claim. Body: 3 short paragraphs or 3 bullet-point facts. Source/attribution ("Dermatology Insights, 2025"). Product visual in sidebar or bottom-right. Brand mark subtle.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Graphic layout with article-style formatting. Product photography as small inset. Typeface choices lean editorial (serif body, sans header).

**Typography role:** The editorial register is carried entirely by typography. Body copy in serif (reads like an article). Bold sans headlines. This format depends on mimicking a publication's visual grammar.

**Where craft lives:** The editorial headline. "This supplement changed my skin" = ad copy. "The ingredient 70% of dermatologists say their patients don't know about" = editorial tension. The format only earns its keep when the headline creates genuine curiosity.

**Texture signal:** Mid-high native feel when typography discipline is tight. Fails when it looks like a designed ad wearing editorial clothes. The best examples are genuinely indistinguishable from content.

---

### 18. Forum / Reddit / Community Screenshot

**Layout:** Screenshot aesthetic — platform chrome (Reddit orange header, Facebook group bar, Slack sidebar). A single post or thread visible. 1–3 comments. The product is mentioned organically in the thread, not featured as a hero.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Screenshot (graphic recreation). Real or realistic platform UI.

**Typography role:** Platform-native fonts. The Reddit post body uses system fonts. The authenticity lives in the interface chrome.

**Where craft lives:** Whether the conversation feels real. A clearly marketing-written post in a forum format = easily spotted. Real or realistic organic-sounding language = the conversion driver. Best examples lift actual customer language from real communities.

**Texture signal:** Very high native feel when executed well. One of the strongest authenticity signals available in static format, because it presents third-party social proof without brand intermediation.

---

### 19. Urgency / LTO Card

*(Near-identical to Format 5 — the distinction is degree of time pressure.)*

**Layout:** Countdown timer graphic or deadline text as primary element. Discount secondary. "Only [N] left" or "Ends in 24 hours" as scarcity signal. Product minimal or absent.

**Texture signal:** Explicitly promotional. Zero native feel. Maximally effective for warm audiences who have already decided; zero effectiveness for cold.

---

### 20. Science / Mechanism Ad

**Layout:** Ingredient or mechanism as headline ("NMN activates your NAD+ pathways"). Molecular graphic or lab-aesthetic visual (test tube, microscope imagery, or clinical trial data). Product as vehicle delivering the mechanism. Dosage or study reference in supporting copy.

**Photography vs. graphic:** Mixed. Clinical photography (lab setting) or flat graphic of molecular structure + product photography. The clinical aesthetic is the credibility signal.

**Typography role:** Technical vocabulary is intentional — it signals that the brand knows the science. Body copy references specific ingredients with doses and (ideally) cited studies.

**Where craft lives:** Mechanism specificity and citation quality. "Clinically proven" (no citation) = ignored. "800mg KSM-66 Ashwagandha — the dosage used in the 2021 Karst et al. stress study" = stops a buyer who has been researching.

**Texture signal:** Mid-spectrum. More credible than pure graphic design, less organic than photography. Clinical aesthetics carry inherent authority that offset the designed appearance.

### DR performance ranking vs. position on axis

Based on practitioner evidence compiled (superscale.ai, segwise.ai, searchengineland.com, vibingsocial.co.uk, favoured.co.uk, foreplay.co):

**Highest-performing for cold/DR traffic:** Native end of spectrum consistently outperforms for cold prospecting.
- Notes App / iMessage / screenshot formats
- Founder photo-led POV
- Forum/community screenshot
- Lifestyle with authentic photography
- Testimonial with real customer photo

**Solid mid-tier DR performers:**
- Us-vs-Them (utility + information = stops scroll for in-market buyer)
- Listicle (cognitive ease drives engagement)
- Problem-Solution (empathy hook)

**Lowest cold-traffic DR performance:**
- Polished designed poster / hero product shot on flat background
- Offer card (works for warm, fails cold)
- Pure infographic (useful but rarely clicks for cold)

### Why the gradient runs this direction

**The core mechanism** (CONFIDENCE: HIGH, based on multiple converging practitioner and research sources):

An ad's appearance on the native↔polished axis determines how much of the buyer's ad-filter reflex fires before they process the message.

Polished, high-production design immediately signals "this is an ad" — the subconscious pattern-recognition trained by years of social media exposure fires within ~200–500ms, before the conscious brain has read a word. The message must then fight uphill against a lowered attention state. (Source: searchengineland.com on ugly ads; favoured.co.uk on Meta's engagement velocity optimization; implicit in Motion's taxonomy noting format signal strength.)

Native-feeling formats exploit a different cognitive pathway. When a viewer sees what appears to be an iPhone Notes screenshot, a friend's text, a Reddit post, or a genuine founder photo, the brain's "threat detection" for advertising either delays or fails to fire. The message gets 1–3 additional seconds of unfiltered attention — which is enormous in a feed environment.

**What specifically gives texture — and why designed posters lose it:**

| Signal | What gives authenticity | What destroys it |
|--------|------------------------|-----------------|
| Photography | Real environments, natural light, identifiable body types, specific scenes | Studio lighting, too-perfect composition, stock-photo models |
| Faces | Real human faces with expression, natural context | Airbrushed/stock headshots, AI-generated faces (uncanny valley) |
| Interface chrome | Real OS fonts (San Francisco, Roboto), real platform UI patterns | Any font substitution, slightly wrong colors |
| Copy voice | First-person, casual, specific, typos OK | Brand voice, marketing cadences, buzzwords |
| Composition | Imperfect, something slightly "off," handheld energy | Perfect centering, even margins, everything at the golden ratio |
| Product representation | Real product photography with real pack/texture | AI-rendered product (wrong shadows, floating edges) |

**Why flat designed graphic posters look generic:**

Three compounding problems:

1. **Category-average aesthetics.** When you design from principles (contrast, hierarchy, brand colors, clean sans-serif) rather than from a specific real-world artifact or scene, the output converges toward the statistical mean of what design tools produce. Every brand using the same tools (Canva, AI generators, stock photo libraries) produces the same aesthetic. The designed poster IS the category average.

2. **Abstraction replaces specificity.** A designed background color communicates nothing specific about the brand's world. A real kitchen, a real factory floor, a real founder's desk — these communicate specificity that cannot be faked. Specificity is what breaks pattern-recognition.

3. **AI generation amplifies the problem.** AI image generators (as of 2026) excel at producing generic-beautiful imagery: abstract designs, futuristic concepts, stylized graphics. They fail at accurate product representation, authentic human emotion, and specific real-world environments. AI-generated product shots have characteristic lighting artifacts that trained eyes identify immediately. (Source: foxwelldigital.com; market-ia.fr; imgix.com research on consumer AI wariness.) An AI-generated designed poster is thus doubly generic: it's a design-poster (already category-average), AND it has AI-generation artifacts (which register subconsciously as "synthetic/untrustworthy"). This is the "AI slop" effect.

**The counter-evidence worth flagging** (CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM):

- Imgix.com research (2025) found that AI-generated imagery "performs just as well — sometimes even better" in controlled tests despite consumer wariness. This suggests the penalty for "AI look" is not universal.
- The 80/20 rule (favoured.co.uk, others): ~80% polished baseline + 20% lo-fi experimentation was the practitioner recommendation, not a wholesale abandonment of designed creative. Some categories (luxury, beauty, premium food) still benefit from high-craft imagery because the product's story IS its production quality.
- Polished hero product shots consistently win for warm/retargeting audiences who already want the product and just need visual confirmation.
- Designed formats like us-vs-them and listicles still perform well despite being clearly designed — because their utility (information delivery) is what drives engagement, not their authenticity.

**The principle that resolves the contradiction:**

Native feel matters most for cold/prospecting traffic — audiences who haven't decided to pay attention and need a reason to stop scrolling. For these audiences, "does this look like something I'd organically encounter?" is the first gate.

For warm/retargeting traffic — audiences who've visited the site, added to cart, or already follow the brand — the native/polished axis matters less. The offer, the proof, or the specific product detail is the decision lever.
