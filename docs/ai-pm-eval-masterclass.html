Chapter 01Why Evals Exist

With traditional software, you know if it works. The button either submits the form or it doesn't. The API returns 200 or 500. You write unit tests. They pass or fail. Binary.

AI broke this. An LLM can:

  • Return a grammatically perfect answer that's completely wrong
  • Give a great answer today and a different one tomorrow on the same input
  • Handle 95% of cases brilliantly and fail catastrophically on the 5%
  • Get worse at Task A when you improve it at Task B

There's no compiler telling you it's wrong. There's no stack trace. The system "works" — it just might work badly. And "badly" is subjective, contextual, and hard to pin down.

Definition
Evals are the replacement for unit tests in a world where outputs are fuzzy. They measure whether an AI system is doing its job well.

Evals Are Taste, Made Measurable

As a PM, you already evaluate AI output. Every time you look at a ChatGPT response and think "hmm, that's not great" — you're doing an eval. In your head. Once. Subjectively.

The problem:

  • You can't do this for 10,000 outputs a day
  • Your taste is in your head, not in the system
  • Your engineers can't optimize for vibes

Evals take your taste — your judgment of what "good" looks like — and turn it into something repeatable, automated, and measurable.

Your taste (subjective)
→
Eval rubric (codified)
→
Automated scoring (repeatable)
→
A number (optimizable)
Key Insight
The PM who defines the eval rubric is defining the product. This is why people say AI PMs need to know evals. It's not a technical skill. It's a product skill wearing a technical costume.

Chapter 02The 4 Types of Evals

1. Capability Evals

"Can it do the thing at all?"

Before you care about quality, you care about basic ability. Can the model follow instructions? Use tools correctly? Stay on topic? Output the right format?

This is table stakes. If you fail here, nothing else matters.

2. Quality Evals

"How well does it do the thing?"

The model does the task, but is the output good? This is the hardest type because "good" depends on your product. As PM, you define "good."

DimensionWhat It MeasuresExample
AccuracyIs the information correct?Does the summary capture the key points?
RelevanceIs it on-topic and useful?Does the recommendation match the user's needs?
CompletenessDid it cover everything?Did the analysis miss any important factors?
ConsistencySame input → similar output?Does it contradict itself across runs?
Tone/VoiceDoes it sound right?Is the customer service bot empathetic enough?
HarmfulnessCould this cause damage?Does it hallucinate medical advice?

3. Safety Evals

"Does it avoid doing bad things?"

Does the model hallucinate? Leak private data? Generate harmful content? Get manipulated by adversarial inputs?

For consumer products, this is existential. One viral screenshot of your AI saying something terrible can kill your company.

4. Efficiency Evals

"Does it do it at acceptable cost and speed?"

The output might be perfect, but if it takes 45 seconds and costs $0.50 per request, your product is dead. Track latency, cost, token efficiency, and number of tool/API calls.

Chapter 03The 3 Approaches to Building Evals

Approach A: Code-Based (Deterministic)

Write regular code to check the output. No AI involved.

// Examples of code-based checks:
Output is valid JSON?         → json.parse()
Response is under 500 tokens? → count tokens
Contains no PII?              → regex for emails, SSNs, phones
Cites sources when asked?     → check for URLs or references
Follows the format spec?      → regex / parsing

Pros: Fast, cheap, deterministic, no false positives.
Cons: Can only check structure, not meaning.
When to use: Always. This is your first line of defense.

Approach B: LLM-as-Judge

Use a (usually stronger) LLM to grade the output of your AI.

// You send to the judge:
"Here's a user question: {question}
 Here's the AI's answer: {answer}

 Rate on a 1-5 scale:
 - Accuracy: Is the information correct?
 - Helpfulness: Does it actually address the question?
 - Conciseness: Is it unnecessarily long?

 Return JSON with scores and reasoning."

The judge doesn't need to be perfect. It needs to correlate with human judgment. If human raters give 4/5 and the judge gives 3.8/5 — that's good enough.

Approach C: Human Evaluation

Actual humans grade the outputs. The gold standard.

Pros: Captures nuance no automated system can.
Cons: Slow, expensive, doesn't scale, humans disagree with each other.
When to use: To calibrate your automated evals. To handle high-stakes decisions.

The Typical Stack

        ┌─────────────────────┐
        │   Human Review      │  ← Monthly on a sample
        │   (calibration)     │     to verify automated evals
        └──────────┬──────────┘
                   │
        ┌──────────▼──────────┐
        │   LLM-as-Judge     │  ← Every change to prompts,
        │   (quality)        │     model, or features
        └──────────┬──────────┘
                   │
        ┌──────────▼──────────┐
        │   Code Checks      │  ← Every build,
        │   (structural)     │     like unit tests
        └─────────────────────┘

Chapter 04The Vocabulary

So you sound smart in meetings.

Benchmark
A standardized eval suite everyone uses to compare models. Like a college entrance exam.
Ground Truth
The known-correct answer for a test case. The answer key.
Rubric
Criteria for judging when there's no single right answer. Like grading an essay.
Inter-Rater Agreement
Do different judges (human or LLM) agree? If 3 teachers grade an essay, do they give similar scores?
Regression
Quality got worse after a change. You fixed feature A but broke feature B.
Contamination
The model was trained on your test data, so scores are inflated. Like giving students the exam questions beforehand.
N-shot
How many examples you show the model before testing. Zero-shot = no examples, few-shot = 2-5 examples.
Elo Rating
Ranking models by head-to-head comparison (like chess). Chatbot Arena uses this to rank models.
Hallucination Rate
How often the model makes up facts. The #1 metric investors and execs ask about.
Win Rate
In A/B comparison, how often does version A beat version B? "Model A wins 62% of the time."

Chapter 05Phase 1: Defining "Good"

This is where most PMs fail. Not because they're dumb, but because they skip this step and jump to tools and metrics. Defining "good" is the hardest, most valuable thing you'll do.

The 5-Question Decomposition Method

Take any output your agent produces and ask these five questions in order:

Q1: What is the USER trying to accomplish?

Not what the agent is doing. What the user needs to happen in their life.

Bad Framing

"The agent should generate 6 hooks"

Good Framing

"A D2C brand manager needs scroll-stopping ad copy they can hand to their media buyer and run TODAY, without rewriting it"

Exercise
For every agent job, write one sentence: "The user needs ____ so they can ____ without ____."

That "without" clause is gold. It tells you what the agent must handle that the user doesn't want to do themselves.

Q2: What would a GREAT human produce?

Not a good human. The best human your user could hire. Your agent should aim for the great human. Your eval should measure distance from the great human, not from the average human.

Why This Matters
Most AI products are evaluated against "did it do the task?" That bar is too low. Users can get that from ChatGPT for free. They're paying you for "did it do the task WELL?"

Q3: What are the FAILURE MODES?

Every AI system has patterns of failure. Name them explicitly:

Failure ModeWhat Happens
The Generic TrapOutput is technically correct but could apply to any company/user/situation. No specificity or differentiation.
The Confidence TrapPresents uncertain information as fact. Doesn't flag assumptions.
The Lazy MiddleThorough on first 60%, rushes through the rest.
The Instruction DriftFollows 8/10 instructions perfectly, silently ignores the other 2.
The Sycophancy TrapIn follow-ups, agrees with everything instead of pushing back when user is wrong.
The Format BreakGreat content in the wrong format. Invalid JSON. Markdown that doesn't render.
The Hallucination BridgeHas 8 real facts, invents 2 more. The real ones make the fake ones look credible.

Q4: What does "barely acceptable" look like?

This is your quality floor. Below this, the output shouldn't reach the user at all. The floor defines your automated guardrails — when output falls below, the system retries, escalates, or shows an error.

Q5: What separates "good" from "exceptional"?

This defines your quality ceiling and tells you where to invest. You won't optimize for exceptional on day one, but knowing what it looks like helps you design evals that detect it.

The "Good Output" Document Template

For each agent job, write one of these:

## [Job Name] — Quality Definition

### User Goal
The user needs _____ so they can _____ without _____.

### Gold Standard
What the best human would produce: [description]

### Known Failure Modes
1. [Failure mode] — how to detect it
2. [Failure mode] — how to detect it

### Quality Floor (minimum acceptable)
- Criterion 1
- Criterion 2

### Quality Ceiling (exceptional)
- Characteristic 1
- Characteristic 2

### Dimensions (what to score)
1. [Dimension]: [what 1 looks like] ... [what 5 looks like]
2. [Dimension]: [what 1 looks like] ... [what 5 looks like]
Key Insight
This document IS your eval rubric. Everything that follows — the code, the judge prompts, the test cases — all flows from this document. If this document is wrong, everything built on top of it will be wrong.

Chapter 06Phase 2: Building the Eval Dataset

Your eval is only as good as the inputs you test on. This is where the "science" of evals lives.

Test Case Anatomy

{
  "input":    // what you feed the agent
  "context":  // any additional info (conversation history, etc.)
  "expected": // properties the output should have (not exact text)
}

The 7 Categories of Test Cases

CategoryPurpose% of Suite
Happy PathNormal, expected use. Establishes baseline.40%
Edge CasesValid but unusual inputs. Finds broken assumptions.20%
AdversarialInputs designed to trick or break the agent.15%
Minimum ViableLeast possible info. Tests if agent asks or guesses.10%
RegressionPast failures you've fixed. "Never again" cases.0% → 15%
Category CoverageOne test per product domain/segment.varies
Comparison PairsSimilar inputs that should produce different outputs.5%
Common Mistake
Testing only happy-path inputs when your real users send typos, ambiguity, and edge cases. Your eval dataset should represent your actual users, not your imagination.

How Many Test Cases?

Starting out:           10–15  (covers happy path + edges + coverage)
After 1 month:          25–30  (add adversarial + minimum + regression)
After 3 months:         50–75  (full coverage + regression cases)
Mature product:         100+   (diminishing returns after 200)

The quality of test cases matters more than quantity. 15 thoughtful cases beat 100 random ones.

Comparison Pairs (Underused, Powerful)

Two similar inputs that should produce different outputs. Tests that the agent actually adapts to context, not running the same template.

Pair A:
  Input 1: "Research nike.com, targeting marathon runners"
  Input 2: "Research nike.com, targeting teenage sneakerheads"
  → Same brand, different ICP. Hooks should be completely different.

Pair B:
  Input 1: "Research supabase.com" (SaaS)
  Input 2: "Research glossier.com" (Beauty)
  → Different categories. Art styles should be different.

Test Case Maintenance

Test cases rot. Quarterly, look at every case and ask:

  1. Is this still a realistic input?
  2. Is the "expected" still correct?
  3. Has the agent been acing this consistently? (If yes, it's not providing signal — replace with a harder case.)

Chapter 07Phase 3: Writing Rubrics That Actually Work

Bad vs. Good Rubric Dimension

Useless

Accuracy: 1 (bad) to 5 (good)

Every grader interprets "bad" and "good" differently. The LLM judge defaults to 4.

Actionable

Each score level has a concrete description, observable criteria, and real examples.

Example: Fully Specified Accuracy Dimension

1
Failing: Multiple hallucinated facts. Key numbers wrong. Claims products or features that don't exist. Example: Says the company has 500 employees when they have 50.
2
Poor: Most facts correct but 2-3 significant errors. Mixes up the company with a competitor on specific points.
3
Acceptable: All major facts correct. Minor imprecisions (rounded numbers, slightly outdated). No fabrication. Example: Says "over 100 employees" when actual number is 127.
4
Good: All facts correct and specific. Numbers match source. Dates and details are precise. No hedging where certainty exists.
5
Exceptional: All facts correct AND includes a verified insight requiring synthesis. Example: "Revenue grew 40% YoY while headcount grew 15%, suggesting strong efficiency gains."

5 Rules for Writing Rubric Dimensions

  1. Every score level needs a concrete description. Not just "better" or "worse."
  2. Include examples at levels 1, 3, and 5. These anchor the grading.
  3. Make levels mutually exclusive. A grader should never think "this could be a 3 or a 4."
  4. The difference between adjacent levels should be observable. "3 = facts correct but generic, 4 = facts correct AND specific" is observable.
  5. Keep it to 3-6 dimensions. More than 6 and grading becomes exhausting. If two dimensions always score the same, merge them.

The 6 Universal Dimensions

These work for almost any agentic product. Pick 3-5 that matter most for yours.

DimensionCore QuestionWhat It Catches
Factual AccuracyCan the user trust the information?Hallucinations, outdated facts, misattributed quotes
Instruction FollowingDid the agent do what was asked?Ignored constraints, wrong format, stopping early
SpecificityIs this uniquely about THIS input?Generic AI slop, template-feeling output
Reasoning QualityDoes the logic hold up?Shallow analysis, unjustified conclusions
Appropriate ConfidenceDoes confidence match evidence?Overconfident hallucinations, missing uncertainty flags
ActionabilityCan the user DO something with this?Information dump with no next steps

Weighted Scoring

Not all dimensions matter equally. The weights ARE your product strategy.

For a B2C Creative Agent:
  Specificity:           30%  (generic = useless)
  Factual Accuracy:      25%  (wrong facts = embarrassing ads)
  Instruction Following: 20%  (6 hooks, right format)
  Actionability:         15%  (paste-ready copy)
  Confidence:            10%  (less critical for creative)

For a B2B Sales Research Tool:
  Factual Accuracy:      30%  (one wrong fact kills credibility)
  Appropriate Confidence:25%  (user's reputation at stake)
  Specificity:           20%  (generic research is worthless)
  Reasoning Quality:     15%  (user needs to understand the "why")
  Actionability:         10%  (user adapts output themselves)
Key Insight
A product that weights accuracy highest is saying "we're the trustworthy option." A product that weights specificity highest is saying "we're the personalized option." Choose deliberately.

Chapter 08Phase 4: LLM-as-Judge Mastery

This is where most implementations fail. Not because the concept is hard, but because the judge prompt is badly written.

The Judge Prompt Structure (5 Parts)

PART 1: ROLE
You are an expert evaluator for [specific domain].

PART 2: CONTEXT
You will be given:
- The user's original input/request
- The AI agent's output
- [Any reference material needed]

PART 3: RUBRIC
Evaluate on these dimensions:
[Full description with examples at levels 1, 3, 5]

PART 4: PROCESS
For each dimension:
1. Quote the specific part that supports your rating
2. Explain why this maps to the score level
3. Assign the score

PART 5: OUTPUT FORMAT
Return your evaluation as JSON:
{ "dimension_1": { "evidence": "...", "reasoning": "...", "score": N } }

6 Techniques That Make Judges Reliable

Technique 1: Chain-of-Evidence Grading

Force the judge to cite evidence FIRST, reason SECOND, score LAST. If it scores first, it rationalizes after.

Bad

"Rate accuracy 1-5."
Judge says "4" with no explanation. You can't debug it.

Good

"First, list every factual claim. Then for each, state if verifiable or hallucinated. Count the ratio. Finally, assign a score."

Technique 2: Anchored Examples

Include real examples of each score level from YOUR product, not abstract descriptions. Dramatically improves consistency.

Technique 3: Adversarial Decomposition

Instead of "is this good?" ask "what's wrong with this?"

// Flaw-counting approach:
"Find flaws. Classify each as:
 - CRITICAL (wrong info, harmful, missed requirement)
 - MODERATE (generic, off-tone, minor gaps)
 - MINOR    (formatting, wordiness, style)

Scoring:
 0 critical + 0-1 moderate = 5
 0 critical + 2-3 moderate = 4
 0 critical + 4+ moderate  = 3
 1 critical                = 2
 2+ critical               = 1"
Why This Works
LLMs are biased toward positive ratings. "Is this good?" defaults to "yes." "What's wrong?" produces actual analysis. Flaw-counting is more calibrated than vibes-based scoring.

Technique 4: Reference Comparison

When you have a known-good output (golden dataset), give it to the judge as a reference. Instead of interpreting your rubric, it has a concrete example to compare against.

Technique 5: Multi-Judge Agreement

Run the same eval with slightly different judge prompts and compare. If they disagree by 2+ points, flag for human review. Mimics "inter-rater reliability."

Technique 6: Pairwise Comparison

Show the judge TWO outputs and ask which is better. Humans and LLMs are both much better at comparing than absolute scoring.

This is how Chatbot Arena works. It's the most reliable eval method we know of.

Chapter 09Phase 5: Structural Evals

No AI needed. Fast, cheap, deterministic. Run on every output, every time. Your first line of defense.

The Complete Structural Checklist

Format Checks

  • Output is valid JSON / Markdown (where expected)
  • Required fields are present and non-empty
  • No [PLACEHOLDER] text remaining
  • No "As an AI language model..." preamble

Length Checks

  • Within expected range (not too short = lazy, not too long = rambling)
  • Individual sections within range
  • Token count within budget

Content Pattern Checks

  • Expected sections/headers present
  • URLs are valid format
  • Dates are valid and recent (not from training data)
  • Numbers are reasonable (no "999,999,999 employees")
  • No duplicate sections

Safety Checks

  • No PII leaked (regex for email, phone, SSN patterns)
  • No competitor disparagement (if policy prohibits)
  • No unauthorized medical/legal/financial advice

Use Structural Evals as Production Gates

Agent produces output
→
Structural eval runs
→
Pass? Show to user
Agent produces output
→
Structural eval runs
→
Fail? Retry or escalate

Chapter 10Phase 6: Running Evals

The Eval Run Flow

For each test case:
  1. Feed input to agent
  2. Collect output (+ intermediate outputs)
  3. Run structural checks → pass/fail array
  4. Run LLM judge → dimension scores
  5. Record: {test_case, output, structural, quality, cost, latency}

After all cases:
  6. Aggregate scores
  7. Compare to previous run
  8. Flag regressions
  9. Generate report

Three Run Modes

ModeWhat It DoesCostWhen
Full PipelineRun agent end-to-endHighBefore major releases, monthly
ComponentFeed pre-made inputs to one stepLowWhen iterating on a specific skill/prompt
CachedRe-evaluate existing outputs with new rubricsMinimalWhen refining evaluation criteria

Most day-to-day work is Component and Cached. Full Pipeline is your weekly/monthly sanity check.

Reading Results: What to Look For

1. Trends (Most Important)

              Week 1   Week 2   Week 3   Week 4
Accuracy:      4.1      4.0      4.2      3.5  ← REGRESSION
Specificity:   3.2      3.5      3.8      4.0  ← IMPROVING
Completeness:  4.5      4.5      4.5      4.5  ← STABLE

2. Distribution (Don't Just Look at Averages)

Scores: [5, 4, 5, 4, 4, 5, 4, 4, 5, 2, 4, 5, 4, 1, 4, 5, 4, 4, 5, 4]

Average: 4.0 (looks fine!)
But: 2 cases scored ≤2 (catastrophic failures hiding in the average)
Watch Out
Users would rather have consistent 3.5s than a mix of 5s and 1s. Always look at the distribution, not just the mean. Variance tells you how reliable your agent is.

3. Per-Category Breakdown

              SaaS    Beauty    Finance
Accuracy:      4.3      4.1      3.4  ← WEAK
Specificity:   4.0      3.8      3.2  ← WEAK

Your agent might excel at SaaS but struggle with finance. Without category coverage, you'd never know.

Chapter 11Phase 7: Production Monitoring

Evals on test cases tell you how your agent should perform. Production monitoring tells you how it actually performs.

Automated Quality Sampling

Take 5-10% of real production outputs. Run your eval pipeline on them. Track over time.

Dashboard:
  Today's samples: 47
  Average quality: 4.1 (↓ 0.1 from yesterday)
  Below floor: 2 (4.2%)  ← investigate these
  Top dimension: Instruction Following (4.5)
  Weakest dimension: Specificity (3.6)

User Signals as Implicit Evals

SignalWhat It Means
Uses output without editingAgent nailed it
Returns for another generationProduct is valuable
Shares/exports the outputHigh quality output
Gives a follow-upTrusts the output enough to build on it
Regenerates immediatelyOutput was bad
Edits heavily after generationClose but not good enough
Abandons mid-generationToo slow or off-track
Doesn't returnProduct didn't deliver value
Key Metric
The regeneration rate is your most important implicit eval. If 30% of users immediately regenerate, your quality bar isn't high enough.

The Closed Feedback Loop

Low score detected
→
Investigate root cause
→
Fix it
→
Add to test suite
→
Verify & deploy

The PM who runs this loop weekly ships a product that gets better every week. The PM who doesn't ships a product that randomly degrades.

Chapter 12Phase 8: Advanced Techniques

Technique 1: Contrastive Evals

Don't just ask "is this good?" Ask "is this better than the alternative?"

Run same 20 test cases on Prompt A (current) and Prompt B (new).
Pairwise comparison for each case: "Which is better?"

Result: "B wins 14/20 cases (70% win rate)"

This is how you make confident decisions about prompt changes.

Technique 2: Ablation Studies

Remove one component and measure the impact. Tells you what's actually driving quality.

Full agent (all steps):              Score: 4.2
Remove research (feed random info):  Score: 2.8  → research adds 1.4 pts
Use generic hooks (skip hook skill): Score: 3.1  → hook skill adds 1.1 pts
Skip art style routing:              Score: 3.7  → art routing adds 0.5 pts

Now you know: research quality is your biggest lever.

Technique 3: Stress Testing

  • Token stress: Brand with an enormous website
  • Speed stress: 10 simultaneous generations
  • Context stress: Follow-up that contradicts the original brief
  • Memory stress: 15 follow-up turns — does quality degrade?
  • Adversarial stress: Inject instructions in the brand's website content

Technique 4: Eval-Driven Development

Evals come FIRST, before code. This is TDD for AI.

Write eval test cases
→
They all fail
→
Build the feature
→
Iterate until pass
→
Ship

Technique 5: Cross-Model Evals

              Haiku 4.5   Sonnet 4.6   Opus 4.6
Quality:       3.8         4.3          4.6
Cost/run:      $0.04       $0.25        $1.50
Latency:       8 sec       22 sec       45 sec
Pass rate:     85%         93%          97%
PM Decision
Is the jump from 3.8 to 4.3 worth 6x the cost? Maybe for B2B (accuracy = trust = revenue). Maybe not for B2C (speed matters more). This is a product decision driven by eval data.

Technique 6: Meta-Evals (Evaluating Your Evals)

  1. Take 30 production outputs
  2. Have 3 humans grade them on your rubric
  3. Run your LLM judge on the same 30
  4. Compute correlation
Correlation > 0.8:   Your judge is reliable. Trust it.
Correlation 0.6-0.8: Decent but some dimensions need work.
Correlation < 0.6:   Judge is measuring the wrong thing. Rewrite.

Do this quarterly. The specific thing to check: does the judge agree with humans on the BAD outputs?

Chapter 13B2C vs B2B: Where Evals Diverge

B2C: Eval for Delight and Safety

B2C users are non-experts. They trust the AI more. They can't catch mistakes as easily.

B2C Priority Stack:
  #1 — Safety / Hallucination      (users can't verify)
  #2 — Calibration                 (must say "I'm not sure")
  #3 — Taste / Tone                (emotional experience IS the product)
  #4 — Completeness                (users expect it "just works")
  #5 — Accuracy                    (important but users are more forgiving)
The "Show Your Friend" Test
Would the user screenshot this and show someone? If yes, your product is working. If they'd be embarrassed to show it, it's not.

B2B: Eval for Precision and Explainability

B2B users are domain experts. They'll catch mistakes. They need to trust the AI enough to stake their professional reputation on it.

B2B Priority Stack:
  #1 — Accuracy                    (experts WILL verify)
  #2 — Reasoning Transparency      (need to know WHY, not just WHAT)
  #3 — Completeness                (partial work = more work for them)
  #4 — Calibration                 (flagging uncertainty shows sophistication)
  #5 — Taste / Tone                (professional, not flashy)
The "Email Your Boss" Test
If a knowledge worker would confidently forward this output to their manager or client as their own work — you win.

Chapter 14Evaluating Agentic Systems

Agentic systems are harder to eval than single-turn chatbots. Here's why and what to do.

The Chain Problem

An agent does 5 things in sequence. The final output might look bad, but where did it go wrong?

            Eval gate       Eval gate       Eval gate
               ↓              ↓              ↓
Input → [Step 1] → out1 → [Step 2] → out2 → [Step 3] → final output
                                                             ↓
                                                    End-to-end eval

Solution: Eval each step independently AND eval the final output. When the final score is low, check which gate failed.

The Tool Use Problem

Agents use tools. The tool might return bad data, and the agent handles it well or poorly.

  • Did it pick the right tool?
  • Did it use the tool correctly? (good search query vs. vague)
  • Did it handle tool failures gracefully?
  • Did it use tools efficiently?

The Multi-Turn Problem

Agentic systems have conversations. Turn 5 depends on Turn 3.

Test case:
  Turn 1: User says "research Nike"
  Turn 2: Agent produces research
  Turn 3: User says "the hooks are too generic, make them edgier"
  Turn 4: Agent revises

  Eval question: Did Turn 4 actually address Turn 3's feedback?
  Or did it just regenerate with slight variations?

The Cost Problem

Track cost per task alongside quality:

           Quality    Cost     Latency
Prompt v1:  3.8       $0.42    45s
Prompt v2:  4.2       $0.38    38s    ← better AND cheaper
Prompt v3:  4.5       $1.20    120s   ← better but 3x cost. Worth it?

That last row is a PM decision, not an engineering decision.

Chapter 15Communicating Evals to Stakeholders

For Executives

Template
"Our agent quality is 4.1/5 this month, up from 3.8 last month. The improvement came from [specific change]. Our biggest risk area is [dimension] at 3.4 — we have a plan to address it by [date]."

Executives want: trend, cause, risk, plan. One paragraph.

For Engineers

Template
"Specificity dropped from 3.9 to 3.4 after the last prompt change. Here are the 5 test cases that regressed — cases 3, 7, 12, 15, 19. The pattern: all are finance brands."

Engineers want: what broke, where, and a hypothesis for why.

For Customers (B2B)

Template
"We evaluate every output across 5 quality dimensions before delivery. Our benchmark suite covers 75 scenarios across 8 industry categories. Our accuracy score is 4.3/5 with a monthly human audit."

Enterprise customers want: rigor, process, proof.

Chapter 16The 10 Commandments of AI Evals

1
You own the rubric. Engineers own the infrastructure.
Don't delegate "what good looks like" to your eng team. They'll optimize for what's measurable, not what matters.
2
Start with 10 test cases, not 100.
A small, well-thought-out test suite beats a large random one. You can always add more.
3
Include adversarial cases.
Don't just test the happy path. Test what happens with bad input, missing data, contradictions. That's where agents break.
4
Eval the chain, not just the end.
When the final output is bad, you need to know which step broke. Eval intermediate outputs too.
5
Calibrate your LLM judge against your own judgment.
Run 20 cases. Score them yourself. Have the judge score them. If you disagree on 30%+, your judge prompt needs work.
6
Version everything.
Rubrics evolve. Test cases change. When scores change, you need to know if it's the agent or the rubric that changed.
7
Don't eval in a vacuum.
"4.2/5" means nothing alone. What matters: "4.2 this week vs 3.8 last week" or "4.2 vs competitor's 4.5."
8
Beware the sycophantic judge.
LLMs are biased toward positive ratings. Use specific failure criteria, include examples of what a 1 looks like, and use "what's wrong?" prompts alongside "rate this."
9
Refresh your test cases quarterly.
If test cases never change, you're testing against history, not reality. Add recent production examples.
10
The eval score is a conversation starter, not a final answer.
A low score means "look at this." Sometimes the eval is wrong. Sometimes the output is creative in a way the rubric can't capture. Use scores to focus attention.
